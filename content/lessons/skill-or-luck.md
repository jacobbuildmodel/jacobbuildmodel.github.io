Since the COE or ERP piece, sealed on 26 September 2026, every prediction I score carries a
confidence: a number I write down before the data is opened, saying how likely I think it is
to hold. Thirteen of them have now been scored. This note is about what those
thirteen can tell me, and what they cannot.

## Calibration: do my numbers mean what they say?

A forecaster is calibrated when the things they give 70 per cent happen about 70 per cent of the
time. The simplest check adds up the confidences. Mine add up to 6.34, so if every number were
exactly right, about six of the thirteen would hold. Eight held. A result that landed in between
counts as not held.

So far, more held than my numbers expected. Taken literally, I was too cautious, not too bold.

## The Brier score: a penalty for confident misses

A total can hide a lot. A forecaster who says 50 per cent on everything, and is right half the
time, is perfectly calibrated and has told nobody anything. The Brier score adds the missing part.
For each prediction, take the gap between the confidence and what happened (1 if it held, 0 if
not), square it, and average. 0 is perfect. Always saying 50 per cent scores 0.25.

My thirteen score 0.179. The squaring is the point: a confident miss costs far more than a
cautious one. I gave one prediction on the Singapore dollar and the ringgit 8 per cent, because I
expected it to fail. It failed, and it cost almost nothing: 0.0064. Had I given it 90 per cent,
the same miss would have cost 0.81, more than a hundred times as much.

## Sample size: why thirteen cannot settle it

Suppose my thirteen confidences were exactly right. Even then, the number that held would not land
on 6.34; it would scatter. Here the standard deviation of that count is about 1.6 predictions,
so eight held sits about one standard deviation above what was expected. A forecaster whose confidences were exactly these, and exactly
right, would see eight or more of the thirteen hold about one time in four.

So the record cannot yet tell me whether I am cautious or lucky. It cannot tell me whether my high
numbers mean what they say, either: one prediction sits at 80 per cent and one at 90, and a single
result says almost nothing about the number behind it. The thirteen run from 8 to 90 per cent, and
no ten-point band holds more than three of them. That is why the record shows each prediction as a
dot and draws no calibration curve.

What it can show is the shape of my mistakes. Every confidence was written before the data was
opened, and every result is published, so the record cannot be flattered after the fact. So far
the misses came where I expected them. Of the five that did not hold, four
failed, none of them with a confidence above 35 per cent; the fifth, at 70, landed in between.

## What I would test next

The test I want is one this record cannot yet run: whether my high numbers hold as often as
they claim. Seven of my confidences are 50 per cent or more, and six of those held, against an
average confidence of 67 per cent. Seven is too few to mean anything. Next, I would fix in advance the count at which I compare the two, and publish the comparison
whichever way it comes out.
