A Certificate of Entitlement is the right to register a vehicle in Singapore and use it for ten
years. The Land Transport Authority decides how many certificates exist and publishes the number
three months at a time, shortly before each quarter begins. That one design choice makes the COE
auction the cleanest lesson in supply and demand I have worked on.

## Supply is fixed before anyone bids

In most markets a higher price draws out more supply. Here it cannot. Every bidding exercise inside
a quarter faces a quota that was announced before the quarter began. No matter how high the price
climbs, not one extra certificate appears. Drawn on a chart, supply is a vertical line.

## So the price traces demand

When supply is vertical, the price that clears the auction is set entirely by what people are
willing to pay for that fixed quantity. Each exercise reports one point on the demand curve. When
LTA moves the quota, it slides the vertical line sideways, and the next price shows where the line
now meets demand.

Two bidding exercises a month have run since 2010, except during a pause from April to June 2020.
That is 393 of these auctions per vehicle category, run by the government, with the results
published.

## What the record shows

Since 2010 the Category A quota has risen 62%, and the average premium, in nominal dollars, 287%.
Compared within each year, 1% more certificates goes with a premium about 0.3% lower. Over the
sixteen years, on this estimate, the larger quota alone implies a premium about 14% lower. The
premium rose anyway, because demand moved further and faster than supply did.

The lesson I take from it is about how to read a price. A high premium looks like a shortage, and
the common argument is that the government keeps cutting the quota. A quota fixed in advance turns
that reading around. With supply set before the bidding, the price is a reading of demand, and a
rising premium on top of a rising quota means demand grew by more than the extra certificates.

The quota is still a real lever, and a weak one. On the same estimate, a premium 10% lower would
take roughly 41% more certificates.

## What the auction cannot teach

A point on a demand curve does not say why the curve moved. I measured what supply explains and
called the remainder demand; I have not measured demand itself. Incomes, population, credit,
foreign buyers and changing preferences all sit in that remainder together.

The quota is also not perfectly fixed over longer spans. It is built from deregistrations, and
owners keep cars longer when premiums are high, which shrinks a later quota. That path is bounded:
83% of the movement in the Category A quota happens inside a single announcement, where prices in
that quarter cannot have touched it. And the method has least to say about the years people care
about most: in 2023 to 2026 the estimate cannot be told from zero.

## What I would test next

The remainder needs a test of its own, sealed before the data is opened. Credit is the cleanest
place to start, because its changes are dated: MAS capped car loans in 2013 and eased the caps in
2016. Next, I would test whether the Category A premium moved after each of those dates by more
than its quota alone implies, with the prediction and my confidence in it sealed first.
