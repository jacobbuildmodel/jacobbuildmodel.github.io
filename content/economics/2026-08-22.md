> In January 2010 a Category A Certificate of Entitlement cost about S$30,000. By 2026 the
> average was $117,548. Over the same sixteen years the government issued 62% more of them.

If you have heard someone say COE prices are high because the government keeps tightening
the quota, you have heard it a lot. It is a reasonable thing to believe. The quota is the
one part of this market a person can point at, the government does set it, and prices did
go up.

The quota also went up. Not down.

That combination is worth explaining, because the auction that sets these prices happens to
be built in a way that lets you check.

## The auction hands you the demand curve

Measuring how much people want something, from market data, usually fails for the same
reason. You see a price and a quantity, but both were settled at once by buyers and sellers
reacting to each other. When the price rises and the quantity falls, you cannot tell whether
you are watching buyers pull back or sellers hold off. Economists call this the
identification problem, and getting around it is most of what applied economics is.

The COE auction solves it by accident.

The Land Transport Authority decides how many certificates exist and publishes the number
three months at a time, one quarter ahead. Every bidding exercise inside a quarter therefore
faces a quota that was announced before the quarter began. No matter how high the price
climbs, not one extra certificate appears. Drawn on a chart, supply is a vertical line.

When supply is vertical, the price that clears the auction is set entirely by what people
are willing to pay for that fixed quantity. Each time LTA moves the quota, it slides the
vertical line sideways, the auction reports back a price, and you get one point on the
demand curve.

{{< figure src="/figs/mechanism.svg" alt="Diagram. Supply is a vertical line because the quota is fixed before bidding opens. Moving that line right slides the clearing price down the old demand curve. The demand curve moved up by more than that, so the price rose instead." caption="Figure 1. The quota fixes the quantity, so the price lands wherever the demand curve is." >}}

Two bidding exercises have run every month since 2010. That is 393 of these experiments per
vehicle category, run by the government, with the results published.

{{< aside title="Why a vertical supply curve solves the identification problem" >}}
The usual problem is that an observed price and quantity are one point where two unknown
curves cross. Move both curves and the points you collect trace out neither one.

Fix the position of one curve and the problem goes away. Every observed point now sits on
the other curve, because the fixed one cannot have moved. That is exactly what a
pre-announced quota does. It is the same logic as a natural experiment, except the
experiment runs twice a month and has done for sixteen years.

The condition it needs is that the quota is set without reference to what bidders are about
to do. LTA sets it from a formula: 25% of the replacement certificates from vehicles
deregistered over the preceding twelve months, plus a growth provision and some adjustments.
Deregistration is a choice people make, so the formula is not completely free of the market.
How far from free is measured in the section on what would prove this wrong.
{{< /aside >}}

## Two numbers in LTA's file are wrong

The bidding results are published through data.gov.sg: 1,965 records from January 2010 to
August 2026, giving the quota, the bids received and the clearing premium for each of five
vehicle categories.

Two records fail a consistency check. In the second exercise of January 2010 the motorcycle
premium is recorded as $20,090, which is the same figure as the category above it in the
same file, and sits between neighbouring months of $889 and $852. The published result that
day was $852. In the first exercise of February 2010 the Category B quota is recorded as
1,154, the same figure as Category A in the same exercise, and about 1.7 times Category B's
quota either side. The published figure was 693.

Both are values copied from the row above. A scan of the whole file found these two and no
others. Everything below uses the corrected data.

{{< aside title="How each correction was checked" >}}
Neither was corrected on the strength of looking wrong. Both were matched against an
independently published result for the same bidding day before being changed.

The Category B error also produces the only exercise in the entire file where fewer bids
arrived than there were certificates available: 930 bids for 1,154 certificates. In an
auction clearing above its reserve price that cannot happen, which is what drew attention
to it.

Five untouched records were spot-checked against published figures and match exactly:
October 2023 exercise 2 Category E at $158,004, September 2025 exercise 2 Category A at
$119,003, November 2025 exercise 1 Category A at $110,002, February 2010 exercise 1 Category
A at $19,989, and the corrected January 2010 Category D at $852.

The cleaning script asserts the original wrong values, so it will fail loudly if LTA ever
fixes the source file.
{{< /aside >}}

## Issue 1% more, and the price falls 0.3%

Start with the picture. Both series indexed to 100 in 2010, on one axis, so the shapes can
be compared directly.

{{< figure src="/figs/indexed.svg" alt="Line chart. Category A quota and premium indexed to 100 in 2010. By 2026 the quota index is 162 and the premium index is 387." caption="Figure 2. Category A quota and premium, both indexed to 100 in 2010." >}}

The estimate is a regression of the log premium on the log quota with year fixed effects,
which means the comparison runs between bidding exercises inside the same year. Anything
that moved slowly across the whole period cannot be producing the result.

For Category A:

> **beta = -0.30**, 95% confidence interval [-0.44, -0.17]

In plain terms: **issue 1% more certificates and the premium falls about 0.3%.**

It is negative, as any demand curve has to be. It is also small. That smallness is the whole
piece.

Before running any of this I made one prediction. Category B is for larger and more powerful
cars, bought by people with more money. For them the certificate is a smaller share of what
they are spending, and the alternatives, taking the train or buying a smaller car, are less
appealing. So Category B buyers should respond less to price, and beta should be larger in
magnitude for B than for A.

It is. The gap is **-0.111**, with a standard error of 0.038 and p = 0.003.

The other half of that is less flattering. Run the same comparison in first differences
rather than levels and the gap shrinks to -0.030, with a p-value of 0.79. Nothing. A result
that holds under one reasonable specification and vanishes under another is a real result
with a real limit on it.

{{< aside title="Four specifications, and why year fixed effects lead" >}}
Category A beta under each:

| Specification | Category A | Category B | Category D |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive levels | -0.246 | -0.456 | **+0.512** |
| Quadratic time trend | -0.320 | -0.613 | -1.114 |
| Year fixed effects | **-0.304** | **-0.432** | -0.703 |
| First differences | -0.157 | -0.187 | -0.228 |

Category D under the naive specification comes out positive, which would mean issuing more
motorcycle certificates raises their price. It does not. Motorcycle premiums rose steeply
over the period for reasons that have nothing to do with quota, and a specification with no
trend in it attributes that rise to whatever else is moving. Add any trend and the sign
flips hard negative. It is a clean example of what omitting a trend does.

Year fixed effects lead because they are the least demanding assumption that still removes
the trend problem. First differences are cleaner in principle and much weaker in practice
here, with an R-squared of 0.01 to 0.03.

Standard errors are Newey-West with 8 lags throughout, which is about four months at two
exercises a month. These are time series and the residuals are autocorrelated.
{{< /aside >}}

{{< aside title="Why I report beta and not the elasticity" >}}
The price elasticity of demand is 1 divided by beta, so Category A's -0.304 implies an
elasticity of about -3.3. Reporting it that way is tempting and fragile.

Dividing by a small, uncertain number produces a very unstable answer. One first-difference
run gave a confidence interval on the elasticity running from -162 to -2.7, which is not a
measurement of anything. The reciprocal looks more like economics and carries less
information. Beta is what was estimated, so beta is what gets reported.
{{< /aside >}}

## The quota should have pushed prices down

Now apply beta = -0.30 to the sixteen years.

| | |
|---|---|
| Category A quota, 2010 to 2026 | 781 to 1,264 per exercise (**+62%**) |
| Average premium, 2010 to 2026 | $30,405 to $117,548 (**+287%**) |
| What the quota change alone predicts | **-13.6%** |

{{< figure src="/figs/decomposition.svg" alt="Number line. What the quota increase alone predicts for the Category A premium is a fall of 13.6 percent. What happened was a rise of 287 percent." caption="Figure 3. Predicted against actual, on one axis." >}}

The extra certificates should have pushed premiums **down** about 14%. They went up 287%.

Supply did not merely fail to explain the increase. It pushed the other way. Whatever moved
prices had to be large enough to absorb a 14% downward push and then deliver a near
fourfold rise on top of it.

2026 is a partial year, fifteen exercises to August. Ending at 2025 instead gives +56% on
quota, +238% on premium, and -12.7% predicted. Where you stop does not change the
conclusion.

There is also a live test. Between 2024 and 2025 the Category A quota rose 24.5%, which
predicts a 6.5% fall in price. Premiums rose 13.7% instead. The additional certificates were
real and demand outran them inside a single year.

Run the arithmetic backwards and you get the number I find hardest to argue with.

> **Cutting Category A premiums by 10% would take roughly 41% more certificates.**
> Confidence interval: +27% to +86%. Under the stricter specification described in the
> section on what would prove this wrong, it takes closer to 99%.

That is the size of the lever everybody is arguing about.

*A note on how this piece came about. I set out to measure an elasticity. The more
interesting result turned out to be what that elasticity implied about sixteen years of
price rises. The Category A against Category B comparison was predicted before the analysis
ran. The decomposition was not, and I think it matters that I say which is which.*

{{< aside title="The decomposition, arithmetic in full" >}}
Working in logs. The total change in the log premium from 2010 to 2026 is +1.352. The change
in log quota is +0.482. Multiplying by beta = -0.304 gives the quota's contribution as
-0.147 in logs, which is -13.6% as a price change.

So the quota contributes -10.8% of a +135.2 log-point move, and everything else contributes
the remaining 110.8%.

The same calculation for the other categories, as the quota rise needed for a 10% price cut:
Category B +28%, Category C +122%, Category D +16%, Category E +32%.
{{< /aside >}}

## The EV test found nothing

Electric vehicles are now a large share of new registrations, and there was a clean policy
change to test against. From May 2022, EVs rated under 110kW became eligible for Category A.
Before that their power output pushed most of them into Category B. That should have moved
demand from B to A and shifted the ratio between the two premiums.

I tested for a break at May 2022 three ways.

| Test | Effect | p-value |
|---|---|---|
| Full sample | -6.4% | 0.24 |
| 2020 to 2024 window | +0.9% | 0.93 |
| 2021 to 2023 window | +11.0% | 0.18 |

None of the three reaches significance at 5%, and the signs disagree.

The narrow window shows +11%, roughly what the hypothesis predicts, and I was pleased with
it for about an hour. Then I ran the same test at dates where nothing happened. **A fake
break in May 2021 produces -23.7%, with a p-value of 0.0004**, far stronger than the real
one.

That placebo is the finding. This series throws off apparent effects of that size at
arbitrary dates, so the test cannot detect anything, and the +11% is noise I would have been
happy to believe. It does not mean the reclassification had no effect. It means this design
cannot tell, probably because EV adoption was gradual rather than sudden, and because the
2022 quota collapse and the post-pandemic demand surge swamp everything else in the data.

The test is here because the alternative was deleting it.

{{< aside title="The placebo tests in full" >}}
Same specification, break date moved to years where no reclassification happened:

| Fake break | Effect | p |
|---|---|---|
| May 2016 | +0.7% | 0.87 |
| May 2018 | -5.6% | 0.50 |
| May 2019 | +9.1% | 0.32 |
| **May 2021** | **-23.7%** | **0.0004** |
| May 2023 | +2.6% | 0.66 |
| May 2024 | +3.3% | 0.50 |

One of six fake dates produces a result far stronger than the real one. That is the
definition of a test without power.
{{< /aside >}}

## What would have to be true for me to be wrong

**The quota is not perfectly exogenous.** It is built from deregistrations, and
deregistration is a choice that responds to price. When premiums are high, owners keep cars
longer, which shrinks the quota a year later. So there is a path running from price back to
supply, and it is worth saying how large it is rather than only that it exists.

Three things bound it. The formula averages deregistrations over twelve months, so a price
shock reaches the quota smeared across a year. The quota is announced a quarter ahead, and
**83% of the movement in the Category A quota happens inside a single announcement**, where
prices in that quarter cannot have touched it. And the channel does not show up in the data:
regressing the change in quota on price changes three to fifteen months earlier gives a joint
p-value of 0.61.

The strongest version of the check is to throw away every comparison that crosses an
announcement. Estimated with quarter fixed effects, so that only quotas published together
are compared, Category A beta is **-0.153** rather than -0.304, still negative and still
significant at p = 0.002. Taking the objection seriously makes the quota look like a weaker
lever, not a stronger one.

**The relationship is not stable across time.** Estimated inside each era separately, beta is
-0.59 in 2010 to 2013, -0.23 in 2014 to 2019, -0.43 in 2020 to 2022, and **+0.14 in 2023 to
2026**. In the most recent stretch the sign flips. That estimate is not statistically
distinguishable from zero, with a p-value of 0.25, so the honest reading is not that the
relationship reverses in the recent era but that within these years the data cannot see it
at all. Either way, the period people care about most is the period where this method has
least to say.

**Category E contaminates the estimates.** Open-category certificates can be used for any
vehicle and in practice get bid up for large cars, which links Category E to A and B through
arbitrage. Treating the five categories as five independent markets overstates how precise
these numbers are.

**Nothing here controls for income, population or credit conditions.** Year fixed effects
absorb anything common to a year, which is a great deal, and not everything.

One more thing is worth putting on the record, because it does not depend on any of the
above being right. For the quota to explain the whole price rise, beta would have to be
**+2.81**. To explain even a quarter of it, +0.70. Those are positive numbers, and a positive
beta means issuing more certificates raises the price. No demand curve does that. Whatever
the bias is and whichever way it runs, it would have to flip the sign and then carry it
several times past zero.

And one that deserves saying plainly. **I have not measured demand.** I measured what supply
explains and called the remainder demand. A residual has a size and it has contents, and
measuring the first is not measuring the second. Incomes, population, credit, foreign buyers,
changing preferences and my own omissions are all in there together, and this analysis cannot
separate them.

Joint test over three to fifteen months: F = 0.85, p = 0.61. The coefficients sum to -0.238,

that fails to reject is not proof the channel is absent, but a channel too small to detect is
also too small to generate a large bias.

peaked at 2,272 in July 2016 with a premium of $53,000. The quota went up by a factor of
6.8 and the premium fell to 0.68 of its level, which implies a beta of -0.203 across that
swing.

## Nine in ten buyers would have to walk away

If premiums are set by demand rather than supply, the question worth asking is what could
move demand for car *ownership*, as opposed to car *use*. Autonomous vehicles are the
obvious candidate, because they change the price of getting somewhere without owning
anything.

I cannot forecast that. Beta does set the bar, though.

Suppose demand falls by the same proportion at every price, so the distribution of bids
keeps its shape and loses a constant fraction of bidders. That is an approximation rather
than a theorem, and it is the assumption doing the work here. Under it, for Category A:

| Price fall | Share of bidders who must stop bidding |
|---|---|
| 5% | 16% |
| 10% | 29% |
| 25% | 61% |
| 50% | **90%** |

For scale, recent Category A exercises attract about 1,921 bids, of which about 1,228
succeed. The other 693, 36% of everyone who bid, go home with nothing and come back next
round.

So halving COE prices would take something like nine in ten Category A bidders giving up
altogether, in a market where more than a third already lose and return anyway. The question
is not whether robotaxis arrive. It is whether they can remove a third of the people
currently willing to pay six figures for a piece of paper.

I do not know, and this data cannot answer it. Answering it would need ride-hailing trip
data, licence-holding by age cohort, and car-sharing membership, none of which appears in a
COE bidding record.

What the bidding records do say is narrower. The quota is a weaker lever than the argument
around it assumes, and for sixteen years it has been pulling in the wrong direction to
explain what happened.

{{< aside title="The proportional-shift assumption" >}}
The table converts a price fall into a share of bidders using beta and the assumption that
demand shifts proportionally: every bidder is equally likely to drop out, whatever they were
willing to pay.

Reality will not be that tidy. If autonomous vehicles appeal most to people who currently
bid low, the bids that disappear are the ones already losing, and the clearing price barely
moves, so the table understates what is needed. If they appeal most to high bidders, it
overstates it. Nothing in a bidding record says which.

The table is a bar to clear, not a forecast.
{{< /aside >}}
