Is getting a COE a lottery? By the auction's rule it is not: certificates go to the highest bids,
and a failed bid is one that ended below the clearing price. What the bidding file adds is how
often that happens. Across 393 Category A exercises since January 2010, 67% of bids succeeded, and
between 53.8% and 75.0% in every full year. Between the best and worst years the win rate varied
1.4-fold and the premium 3.9-fold. The win rate is slightly lower when premiums are high. These
are bids, not bidders.

## The mechanism: the quota fixes how many bids win, so bidding decides the price

The quota exists to cap the number of vehicles. It also fixes, before bidding opens, how many
bids can succeed in an exercise. Bidders respond on price, by raising a reserve price or holding
it. A count of failed bids leaves out that the number of winners was never in play: what bidding
decides is the price, and which bidders are above it.

A Certificate of Entitlement is the licence you need before you can register a car here. The
government decides how many exist, and you bid for one twice a month. Since 2010 the price of
a Category A certificate has nearly quadrupled.

When something gets that much more expensive, the natural assumption is that it also got
harder to get. That is the part worth checking, because the bidding file records both the
price and the number of bids, so the question does not need a model to answer it.

## The belief: a COE is a ballot that most people lose

The belief is that a COE is something you queue for and mostly do not get, the way people
talk about a BTO ballot.

That is a reasonable thing to think, and it is worth saying why before taking it apart. The
government fixes how many certificates exist and announces the number in advance. You submit
a bid, you wait a week, and you find out. Structurally it looks a lot like a draw. People
really do fail and come back the following exercise, and the ones who fail are the ones who
talk about it.

So the belief is not silly. It is built on the visible part of the process.

## The win rate: two in three Category A bids succeed

The bidding file records how many bids arrived and how many succeeded, so the question needs
no modelling.

Across all 393 Category A exercises since January 2010, **67% of bids succeeded**. The lowest
any full year managed was 53.8%, in 2013. The highest was 75.0%, in 2017.

Motorcycles are the easiest category at 76%. Open certificates are the hardest at 60%, which
makes sense, because they can be used for any vehicle and so attract bidders from everywhere
else.

The extremes are worth knowing, because they are further apart than the averages suggest. The
hardest exercise on record was March 2013, when 329 of 1,180 bids won, a rate of 27.9%, at a
premium of $74,689. The easiest was October 2018, when 1,672 of 1,811 won, 92.3%, at $25,556.

In recent exercises about 1,777 people bid for Category A, about 1,128 get a certificate, and
about 649 do not.

Which leaves the harder question. If two in three bids have been winning for sixteen years,
something else has been absorbing the pressure.

## Premium against win rate: relative to its average, the premium varies about five times as much

A fixed quota has to ration certificates somehow, and there are only two ways to do it. Fewer
people can win. Or the price can rise.

Compare how much each one actually moved. Between the best and worst years, the Category A
win rate varied by a factor of 1.4. The premium varied by a factor of 3.9. Measured as
variation relative to the average, the premium moves about five times as much as the win
rate does.

That pattern holds in every category. For all five, the premium's variation is between four
and seven times the win rate's.

So the auction rations mostly through price, and hardly at all through luck. **A failed bid
is not bad fortune. It is a bid below the clearing price.**

This follows from how the auction is built. The quota fixes the quantity before bidding
opens, so quantity cannot absorb any pressure. Price is the only thing left with room to
move, and it moves.

## The complication: the win rate is slightly lower when premiums are high

The honest complication is that the win rate is not completely flat. It is slightly lower
when premiums are high. Across all 393 Category A exercises the correlation is -0.15, with a
p-value of 0.002, so it is small but real. On annual averages it is -0.33 with a p-value of
0.20, which is not distinguishable from nothing with only seventeen years.

So price is not doing quite all of the work. It is doing most of it.

If that correlation were strong, say below -0.5, the ballot framing would have more in it
than I am giving it credit for. And if a future stretch showed the win rate falling sharply
while premiums held flat, the rationing would have switched from price to queue, and this
piece would be wrong about the mechanism rather than just the size.

## Bids, not bidders: what the file cannot say

**These are bids, not bidders.** The file counts bids submitted, not people. Someone who
fails and comes back appears twice. So the share of *people* who eventually get a certificate
is higher than 67%, and this data cannot say how much higher. If you are patient, your odds
are better than the headline number, and I cannot tell you by how much.

**Most bids are placed by dealers on a buyer's behalf.** So a bid is not always one person
deciding to buy a car, and the count is not a clean count of demand.

**None of this says the price is reasonable.** A market where everybody won at $200,000 would
be worse, not better. This measures how the market rations, not whether the outcome is one
anybody should be happy with.

**Tested here:** what share of bids succeeds, how much that share varies beside the premium, and
whether it falls when premiums are high (slightly).

**Not tested here:** why about two in three bids succeed year after year. The share is the quota
over the bids received, so it depends on how many bids are entered for each certificate. Under
LTA's rules a bidder whose reserve price the current price passes is out unless the reserve is
revised upwards ([LTA](https://onemotoring.lta.gov.sg/content/onemotoring/home/buying/upfront-vehicle-costs/certificate-of-entitlement--coe-.html)). The file holds no record of single bids or of who placed them,
so it cannot show what keeps the number of bids in step with the quota.

## The reading: the premium moved several-fold while the win rate stayed near two in three

The COE is not a ballot. It is an auction that clears, and it clears by price rather than by
turning people away. Two in three bids have been winning a certificate for sixteen years,
through a period when the premium nearly quadrupled. The queue stayed roughly the same length
because the price did the adjusting instead.

That reframes what a rising premium means. It is not evidence that certificates got scarcer or
that the process got more competitive. It is evidence that the people still bidding were willing
to pay more, and that the auction found the number at which enough of them dropped out.

If you are watching this yourself, the number to watch is the success rate rather than the
headline premium. A premium rising while the win rate holds is the ordinary state of this
market. A premium rising while the win rate falls would be something new, and it would mean
the rationing had started happening somewhere other than the price.

*A historical reading of public data, about what happened, not a forecast. It is not
advice, and nothing here is tailored to any reader's own vehicle or bid.*
