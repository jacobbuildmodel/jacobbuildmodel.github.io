> Ask anyone who has bought a car here and you will hear about the COE they did not get. It
> sounds like bad luck. Across 393 bidding exercises since 2010, two in three Category A bids
> succeeded, and that share has barely moved while the price nearly quadrupled.

## Everyone I know calls it a ballot

The belief is that a COE is something you queue for and mostly do not get, the way people
talk about a BTO ballot.

That is a reasonable thing to think, and it is worth saying why before taking it apart. The
government fixes how many certificates exist and announces the number in advance. You submit
a bid, you wait a week, and you find out. Structurally it looks a lot like a draw. People
really do fail and come back the following exercise, and the ones who fail are the ones who
talk about it.

So the belief is not silly. It is built on the visible part of the process.

## Two in three bidders get one

The bidding file records how many bids arrived and how many succeeded, so the question needs
no modelling.

Across all 393 Category A exercises since January 2010, **67% of bids succeeded**. The lowest
any full year managed was 53.8%, in 2013. The highest was 75.0%, in 2017.

Motorcycles are the easiest category at 76%. Open certificates are the hardest at 60%, which
makes sense, because they can be used for any vehicle and so attract bidders from everywhere
else.

The extremes are worth knowing, because they are further apart than the averages suggest. The
hardest exercise on record was March 2013, when 329 of 1,180 bids won, a rate of 27.9%, at a
premium of $74,689. The easiest was October 2018, when 1,672 of 1,811 won, 92.3%, at $25,556.

In recent exercises about 1,777 people bid for Category A, about 1,128 get a certificate, and
about 649 do not.

## The price moves so the queue does not

A fixed quota has to ration certificates somehow, and there are only two ways to do it. Fewer
people can win. Or the price can rise.

Compare how much each one actually moved. Between the best and worst years, the Category A
win rate varied by a factor of 1.4. The premium varied by a factor of 3.9. Measured as
variation relative to the average, the premium moves about five times as much as the win
rate does.

That pattern holds in every category. For all five, the premium's variation is between four
and seven times the win rate's.

So the auction rations mostly through price, and hardly at all through luck. **A failed bid
is not bad fortune. It is a bid below the clearing price.**

This follows from how the auction is built. The quota fixes the quantity before bidding
opens, so quantity cannot absorb any pressure. Price is the only thing left with room to
move, and it moves.

## What would prove this wrong

The honest complication is that the win rate is not completely flat. It is slightly lower
when premiums are high. Across all 393 Category A exercises the correlation is -0.15, with a
p-value of 0.002, so it is small but real. On annual averages it is -0.33 with a p-value of
0.20, which is not distinguishable from nothing with only seventeen years.

So price is not doing quite all of the work. It is doing most of it.

If that correlation were strong, say below -0.5, the ballot framing would have more in it
than I am giving it credit for. And if a future stretch showed the win rate falling sharply
while premiums held flat, the rationing would have switched from price to queue, and this
piece would be wrong about the mechanism rather than just the size.

## What this does not tell you

**These are bids, not bidders.** The file counts bids submitted, not people. Someone who
fails and comes back appears twice. So the share of *people* who eventually get a certificate
is higher than 67%, and this data cannot say how much higher. If you are patient, your odds
are better than the headline number, and I cannot tell you by how much.

**Most bids are placed by dealers on a buyer's behalf.** So a bid is not always one person
deciding to buy a car, and the count is not a clean count of demand.

**None of this says the price is reasonable.** A market where everybody won at $200,000 would
be worse, not better. This measures how the market rations, not whether the outcome is one
anybody should be happy with.
