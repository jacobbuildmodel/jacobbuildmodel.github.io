In June 2017, one in four of Singapore's landscape workers earned a basic wage
of S$1,300 a month or less, and at that mark pay sat exactly on the first rung
of the wage ladder written into the rules for registered landscape firms a year
earlier. In June 2018 the mark was S$1,300 again. By June 2022 the rung had
risen to S$1,550, and so had pay at the mark: S$1,550, to the dollar.

Landscape pay at the bottom pulled ahead once the ladder bound. Yet for
cleaners and security guards, whose ladders are the best known, the record
cannot say what happened, because their pay was already moving before the
ladders arrived.

In September I wrote down four bets on what the public wage survey would show,
with how sure I was of each, and put them on an
[open question page](/open/2026-09-28-minimum-wage-or-pwm/) before opening the
data. Three held. This is what they found, where I was wrong, and the part of
the question public data cannot reach.

## The argument: one floor for everyone, or a ladder for each job

Singapore has no minimum wage. From 2014 it put wage ladders, a floor for each
step up in skill, into the licences of cleaning firms and security agencies,
and into the registration of landscape firms. The case for a national minimum wage is that nobody at the
bottom waits for their industry's turn. The case for the ladder is that jobs
differ, and a floor cut to fit each one can be set high where the job can bear
it. The yardstick here is pay at the bottom of each job: the 25th percentile of
monthly wages in the Ministry of Manpower's June Occupational Wage Survey, the
pay at the one-in-four mark, which a quarter of workers earn or less.

## Theory and the extremes: a uniform or a tailor

In the textbook market, a floor set above what a job is worth costs jobs. An
employer with few rivals, what economists call a monopsony, pays less than a
worker is worth, and there a floor can raise pay and jobs together. Pushed to
the extreme, a minimum wage is one school uniform for every student. It fits
some, pinches others, and some leave, but it covers every low-paid job at
once. A ladder is a tailor who makes perfect suits, but
only for three classes: the covered jobs get a floor that fits, and everyone
else gets nothing.

## My bets: four predictions, sealed before the data

The covered jobs were compared with low-paid jobs that got no ladder until
2022 or 2023: shop assistants, cashiers, waiters, kitchen and food stall
assistants, office clerks, and van and lorry drivers.

1. **They moved together before the ladders** (28 per cent). A design test: if
   covered and comparison pay was already drifting apart by more than 1.0 per
   cent a year, the comparison is unfair for that job.
2. **The suit fits** (40 per cent). Bottom pay in covered jobs rose at least
   10.5 per cent more than in comparison jobs.
3. **The rest kept pace** (58 per cent). Bottom pay in jobs without a ladder
   grew no more than about 5.1 per cent less than the median worker's.
4. **The first rung shows** (50 per cent). Pay at the one-in-four mark was at
   least the ladder's entry wage every year.

A fifth bet, on whether the covered jobs survived, could not be scored.

## What the data said: landscape pay pulled a quarter ahead

![Bottom pay in cleaning, security and landscape jobs against jobs without a ladder, every June 2009-2025](/figs/pwm_chart1_gap.svg)

Before its ladder, landscape pay at the bottom moved in step with the
comparison jobs: a drift of 0.1 per cent a year, well inside my line of 1.0 per
cent. After the ladder bound in 2016, it pulled ahead. Across the years
compared, landscape workers' bottom pay gained 24.6 per cent on the comparison
jobs, with a 90 per cent interval of 17.1 to 32.6 per cent. The bet needed 10.5
per cent. It held.

## Where I was wrong: the design test failed for cleaners and guards

**The design test failed, as I expected it might.** I gave it 28 per cent. For
security guards, pay at the bottom was already pulling ahead of the comparison
jobs before their ladder, by about 2.5 per cent a year, more than double my
line. For cleaners, the series was too short and too noisy: four years before
the ladder, falling behind by about 3.0 per cent a year, with an interval wide
enough to hold almost anything. Under the rule I set, both jobs dropped out of
the pay bet and the rung bet. Their lines are on the chart, but their steps
before and after are not findings.

**Then I underrated three bets.** The pay bet held, at 40 per cent. The rung
bet held, at 50 per cent. And the bet that jobs without a ladder kept pace held,
at 58 per cent, by the narrowest margin of the four.

**The tally.** 3 of 4 held against the 1.76 I expected. The Brier score, which
measures how far each confidence sat from what happened, was 0.216. A
forecaster who always says 50 per cent scores 0.25, and lower is better. My
confidence was too low on three bets and right to be low on one. Across both
pieces so far, 4 of 8 predictions have held against 3.66 expected, with a
Brier score of 0.205: a little better than the coin-flip mark of 0.25, on far
too few bets to judge anyone's calibration.

## The obvious objection: landscape pay might have risen anyway

Maybe landscape pay was about to rise whatever happened. The design test is the
answer to that: before the ladder, landscape pay was not drifting away from the
comparison jobs. The objection has a stronger form, though. Some versions of
the test did show a drift. The steepest, on the years after the last change in
job titles, was 1.2 per cent a year. Carry that drift forward the 7.5 years
between the middle of the before years and the middle of the after years, and
it accounts for 9.3 per cent. What is left is a gain of 14.0 per cent, still
above the 10.5 per cent the bet needed. This check was added after the results
and is not scored.

## Where it flips: two results that sit on a knife edge

**Landscape only just passed the design test.** Its drift was 0.1 per cent a
year on the years I sealed. Leaving out June 2009, or using only the years
after the last change in job titles, puts it just over the 1.0 per cent line.
Those versions are sensitivities, reported and not scored, but the one job
carrying the pay and rung bets passed its design test narrowly.

**The rest of the bottom kept pace by half a point.** Pay in the jobs without a
ladder fell short of the median by 4.6 per cent, against a line of 5.1 per
cent. The median I sealed excludes employers' contributions to CPF, Singapore's
compulsory savings scheme, because the wage survey's gross pay excludes them
too; that keeps the two like for like. Measured against the median including
those contributions, the shortfall was 6.4 per cent, and the bet would have
failed. That second check was also
added after the results, and it is not scored.

## The floor becomes the wage: pay sat exactly on the rung

![Landscape workers' basic pay at the one-in-four mark as a share of the ladder's entry rung, 2017-2022](/figs/pwm_chart3_rung.svg)

In three of the four years compared, landscape pay at the one-in-four mark was
the entry rung to the dollar: S$1,300 in 2017 and 2018, and S$1,550 in 2022. In
2019 it was S$1,350, just above. The rung bet held, since no year fell below
it. But the more telling fact is where the pay sat. For a large share of the
lowest-paid landscape workers, the ladder's first step was not a floor under
their pay. It was their pay: the floor became the wage.

## The rest of the bottom: jobs without a ladder kept pace, just

![Bottom pay in jobs without a ladder against the median worker's income, 2009-2019](/figs/pwm_chart2_pace.svg)

From 2010-12 to 2017-19, bottom pay in the comparison jobs grew 28.0 per cent,
and the median worker's income grew 33.9 per cent. The gap between them stayed
under my line, so the tailor's cost, the rest of the bottom left behind, did
not show on the sealed measure. These jobs were not without a floor: firms that
hired foreign workers had to pay each local at least a set salary for that
local to count fully toward their quota, and that floor rose through the
decade.

## What would change my mind

- A yearly public count of cleaners, security guards and landscape workers,
  from before the ladders, would let the question of jobs be tested at last.
- The wage survey's 2007 and 2008 tables, held only as PDFs, could give
  cleaners enough years before their ladder to pass or fail the design test on
  firmer ground.
- A drift before the ladder in landscape pay, confirmed in those earlier
  tables, would turn its gain into a trend it was already on.

## The verdict: pay rose where the ladder was hung; the jobs question is open

The record cannot tell the two models apart; the rest of the bottom kept pace.

Singapore publishes no yearly count of cleaners, security officers or landscape
workers, so whether the ladders cost jobs cannot be tested from public data.

The pay test leaned against finding a gain: in-house workers, who were not
covered until 2022, sat inside every job's numbers, and the quota rule set a
floor under the comparison jobs too. So the gain found for landscape workers is strong
evidence. The pace test leaned the other way: that same floor helped the jobs
without a ladder keep up, so "kept pace" is weak evidence.

## What this does not explain: jobs, a national floor, small firms

It does not explain whether the ladders cost jobs, because the count does not
exist. It says nothing about what a national minimum wage would have done,
since Singapore never had one. The wage survey covers only firms with 25 staff
or more, so hawker stalls and small shops are outside it. And in-house
cleaners, guards and gardeners, employed directly rather than by a contractor,
sit inside the numbers although their ladder came only in 2022.

## What to watch next

Shop, food, waste, office and driving jobs got their ladders in 2022 and 2023.
As later wage surveys cover them, does pay at their one-in-four mark settle
exactly on the first rung too? Questions like this one are listed, with their
dates, on the [open questions page](/open/).
