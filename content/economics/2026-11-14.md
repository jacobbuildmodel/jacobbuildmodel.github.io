S$1,000 bought about 78,000 yen in January 2021 and about 120,000 in December
2025, at the Monetary Authority of Singapore's (MAS's) rates of S$1.28 and
S$0.83 per 100 yen. Tokyo did get cheaper. Did Singapore get richer? Its dollar
did rise, but most of the change was the yen's own fall: in log terms, about
70% of the rise was the yen falling against everyone and about 30% the
Singapore dollar's own slow climb, with a small remainder.

In 39 of the 46 overlapping five-year stretches since 2005 when the Singapore
dollar gained on the yen, the yen's fall was the larger part (added after
outside review, not sealed). Over two decades, measured against everyone, the
Singapore dollar's climb added up to more; against the US dollar alone, the
order reverses.

Who moved is not who benefits: whichever currency moved, a Singaporean's dollar
bought the full gain in yen.

Measured from single months at each end, the rise is 54 per cent; the tests
average three months at each end, which gives 49.5. I sealed five bets on the
split on an [open question page](/open/2026-10-03-singapore-dollar/) before
opening the data. Four held. The one I expected to fail, on the ringgit,
failed.

## The argument: strength, delivered through steering

The comfortable story says a strong economy makes a strong currency. But since
1981 MAS has steered the Singapore dollar inside an unpublished band that, its
framework FAQ says, crawls "at different rates over time, depending on current
and future expected price developments in the economy", which the output gap
feeds. Such a currency's long-run rise is commonly linked to productivity
growth (Balassa 1964; Samuelson 1964) and to an anti-inflation stance. So
steering sets the speed and fundamentals shape the direction: strength,
delivered through steering. That is theory, not a finding here. A rate moves
when either side moves, so each side is measured against everyone. At the
extremes, Hong Kong's dollar rides in the US dollar's car; the yen, floating
since 1973, is a boat without an anchor; Singapore is on cruise control.

## My bets: five predictions, sealed before the data

Each currency's broad index, from the Bank for International Settlements, is
its value against 64 economies' currencies, weighted by trade.

1. **The split adds up** (55 per cent): the remainder within 20 per cent of the
   move on both windows, 2021-25 and August 2005 on (after the ringgit's and
   the renminbi's pegs to the US dollar ended in July 2005), and BIS rates
   within half a per cent of MAS's.
2. **The yen's share** (90 per cent): at least half the rise was the yen's
   fall, and more than the Singapore dollar's own rise.
3. **The ringgit's share** (8 per cent): the same, for the ringgit.
4. **The slow path** (80 per cent): the steadiest or second steadiest broad
   index of 11: the Singapore dollar, yen, ringgit, won, renminbi, baht,
   rupiah, US dollar, euro, Australian dollar and Hong Kong dollar.
5. **Policy, not growth** (35 per cent): its path lined up clearly better with
   MAS's decisions than with growth.

## What the data said: nearly three-quarters was the yen's fall

![For the yen and the ringgit, three moves in per cent from early 2021 to late 2025: the Singapore dollar against everyone, the partner against everyone, and the Singapore dollar against the partner](/figs/sgd_chart1_split.svg)

The Singapore dollar's broad index rose 13 per cent; the yen's broad index fell
25 per cent. Of the 49.5 per cent rise against the yen, 73 per cent was the
yen's fall and 31 per cent the Singapore dollar's rise, with 4 per cent the
other way; as log shares, the three add up exactly. The bet held. So did the
first: the split added up, and BIS rates matched MAS's.

Against the US dollar instead, the yen's share was even larger, 93 per cent,
while the ringgit's split depended on what it was measured against. The yen
went from about 105.9 to 154.1 per US dollar (three-month averages), a fall
commonly linked to the gap between Japanese and US interest rates; the Bank
of Japan ended negative rates only in March 2024. Adjusted for prices, the
yen's 2025 average against its trading partners was the second lowest of
32 full years since 1994, just above 2024's (all added after outside review,
not sealed).

## Five-year stretches: mostly the yen, but not over two decades

![Every five-year window since August 2005: the Singapore dollar's own rise and the yen's own fall as shares of the Singapore dollar's rise against the yen, with the windows where it did not rise shaded](/figs/sgd_chart4_rolling.svg)

The chart splits every five-year window from August 2005, in three-month steps
(added after outside review, not sealed); consecutive windows share 57 of 60
months, so they are not 46 independent results. Against everyone, from August
2005 the Singapore dollar rose 87 per cent against the yen: its own rise 57 per
cent of the move, the yen's fall 51, a remainder of -8. That long split was
sealed as context.

## What failed, as I expected: the ringgit rose against everyone

I gave the ringgit bet 8 per cent, expecting the strong ringgit of 2024 and
2025 to end the window higher against everyone. It did. Both currencies rose
against the world, and the Singapore dollar rose a little more. That small gap
is the whole 5%. That is two sides to every rate: against the yen, the yen
moved most; against the ringgit, the Singapore dollar.

**The tally.** 4 of 5 held against the 2.68 I expected, the five confidences
added up. What is scored is each confidence, not the hit rate: the Brier score,
how far each sat from what happened, was 0.136, against 0.25 for always saying
50 per cent (lower is better). Saying 8 per cent and seeing that bet fail
counted in my favour.

## The obvious objection: isn't steering just strength?

MAS's slope partly tracks the economy, so a path that follows MAS might
follow growth one step removed. Over the 61 stretches between decisions, the
path's rank correlation with MAS's decisions was 0.47 (1 is the same order, 0
no relation), and with growth 0.11. MAS's lead, 0.35, beat the pass line of
0.20, but its 90 per cent range, 0.11 to 0.59, reaches below that line, so the
lead is not clear of chance. The bet held, at 35 per cent; a finer coding of
the slope gave 0.346. A correlation of only 0.47 also means the band left real
room to move.

## Where it flips: inflation lined up as well as MAS did

With CPI inflation in place of growth the result turns over: a rank correlation
of 0.51, and MAS's lead -0.04, a sealed sensitivity, not scored. Beside it sits
the sentence sealed with the bet:

> The design leans toward the rival. The band is the path MAS keeps the index
> on, so a score that describes the band will match the index's path wherever
> MAS held the index inside it. A result that the path followed MAS's
> decisions is weak evidence that MAS rather than the economy moved the
> Singapore dollar. A result that it followed growth at least as closely is
> strong evidence against the rival.

## The world view: steadier even than Hong Kong's dollar

![The typical monthly move of each currency's broad index since August 2005, eleven currencies ranked from steadiest to least steady](/figs/sgd_chart2_steady.svg)

Since August 2005 the Singapore dollar's broad index moved about 0.47 per cent
in a typical month (the standard deviation of the monthly change), the
steadiest of the 11; the bet held. Hong Kong's came second, at 1.00 per cent,
by design: its dollar is pegged to the US dollar alone, between HK$7.75 and
HK$7.85, so its value against a basket swings with the US dollar's (about 0.78
of the swing, a correlation of 0.92 on monthly changes), while MAS manages
against a basket. The yen was the least steady, at 2.16 per cent.

## The MAS path: a slow line with turns at the decisions

The BIS index moved with MAS's own with a correlation of 0.92, also on monthly
changes, so it describes the index MAS steers.

![The Singapore dollar's broad index since 2001, with each MAS decision marked beneath and GDP growth over each stretch between decisions](/figs/sgd_chart3_path.svg)

The index climbed slowly under a rising slope, went flat or dipped after the
easings of 2009 and 2020, and climbed steepest in 2022, when MAS also lifted
the whole band. MAS reduced the slope in January and again in April 2025, so
the Singapore dollar was being steered flatter as the window closed.

## What would change my mind

- A later five-year window where the Singapore dollar's own rise outweighed
  the yen's fall.
- A broad index that swung like a floating currency's.
- A path that lined up with growth as closely as with MAS's decisions.

## The verdict: mostly the yen; the path lined up with MAS

Most of the Singapore dollar's rise against the yen was the yen falling against
everyone; most of its rise against the ringgit was the Singapore dollar rising
against everyone; its broad path followed MAS's decisions more closely than
growth.

With CPI inflation in place of growth, MAS's lead was -0.04, and -0.01 with
inflation a quarter ahead (added after outside review, not sealed), so the
record cannot separate MAS's decisions from the inflation MAS targets.

Weaker than they read:

- **The ringgit:** the two countries weigh heavily in each other's baskets,
  so it was sealed as weaker evidence.
- **The policy result:** sealed as weak evidence; the band is drawn around
  the path.
- **The yen:** true of the recent window and most five-year stretches; over
  two decades, against everyone, the Singapore dollar's own rise was larger.

## What it changes: the cheap yen was mostly Japan's story

The evidence cut against reading the cheap yen as a sign of Singapore's
strength: in most five-year stretches, a cheaper Tokyo was mostly a weaker yen.
It helped the steering story less, since inflation matched the path as well as
MAS did. Weighing "strong economy, strong dollar" now means counting the yen's
swings, which dominated most five-year stretches; the Singapore dollar's climb,
which, against everyone, dominated two decades; and the gain in yen, which went
to Singaporeans whoever moved.

## What this does not explain: prices, flows and motive

Japan's consumer prices rose 13 per cent, so a Singapore dollar bought about 32
per cent more Tokyo goods, less than the 49.5 per cent more yen. The real
exchange rate rose more, 54.8 per cent, because Singapore's prices rose faster,
17 per cent; the yen's own fall was still 71 per cent of that real rise (added
after outside review, not sealed). Nothing here tests interest rates, capital
flows or MAS's motives. I knew the yen had fallen since 2021 when the window's
rule was set, and disclosed it with the sealed bets.

## What to watch next

If the yen recovers, for instance as Japan's interest rates rise, the same
trip costs more again, and that would show in the yen's own index, not the
