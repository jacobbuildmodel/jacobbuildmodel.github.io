In January 2021, MAS's monthly average rate was S$1.28 for 100 yen, so a
S$1,000 trip budget bought about 78,000 yen. In December 2025 the rate was
S$0.83, and the same budget bought about 121,000 yen. Tokyo did get cheaper.
Did Singapore get richer? Its dollar did rise, but most of the change was the
yen's own fall.

Single months overstate the move a little, so the tests average three months
at each end, January to March 2021 against October to December 2025. On that
measure the Singapore dollar rose 49.5 per cent against the yen. Its broad
index, its value against everyone, rose 13 per cent. The yen's fell 25 per
cent.

In October I sealed five bets on how that rise would split, each with a
confidence, on an [open question page](/open/2026-10-03-singapore-dollar/),
before opening the data. Four held. The one I expected to fail, on the
ringgit, failed.

## The argument: strength or steering

The comfortable story says a strong economy makes a strong currency, so a cheap
yen is a sign of Singapore doing well. The steering story says the Singapore
dollar does not float. Since 1981 MAS has kept it inside an unpublished band
against a basket of trading partners' currencies, and usually lets it climb
only slowly. If that is right, a cheap yen says more about Japan than about
Singapore.

A rate against one currency is a ratio, so it moves when either side moves.
The only way to tell who moved is to measure each side against everyone else.

## The extremes: a passenger and a boat

Hong Kong is the extreme of steering: its dollar is held between HK$7.75 and
HK$7.85 per US dollar, a passenger in the US dollar's car. Japan is the
extreme of drifting: the yen has floated since 1973, a boat without an anchor.
Singapore sits between them, on cruise control inside a lane, with MAS
resetting the speed at each review.

## My bets: five predictions, sealed before the data

The Bank for International Settlements publishes a broad index for each
currency, its value against 64 economies' currencies weighted by trade. With
those, a rise against the yen splits into three parts: the Singapore dollar
rising against everyone, the yen falling against everyone, and a remainder
neither index accounts for.

1. **The split adds up** (55 per cent). The remainder stays within 20 per cent
   of the move, for the yen and the ringgit, on both windows, and the BIS rates
   match MAS's own to within half a per cent on average.
2. **The yen's share** (90 per cent). At least half the rise against the yen
   was the yen falling, and more of it than the Singapore dollar's own rise.
3. **The ringgit's share** (8 per cent). The same, for the ringgit.
4. **The slow path** (80 per cent). The Singapore dollar's broad index was the
   steadiest or second steadiest of 11 currencies.
5. **Policy, not growth** (35 per cent). Its path lined up clearly better with
   MAS's decisions than with Singapore's growth.

## What the data said: nearly three-quarters was the yen's fall

![For the yen and the ringgit, three moves in per cent from early 2021 to late 2025: the Singapore dollar against everyone, the partner against everyone, and the Singapore dollar against the partner](/figs/sgd_chart1_split.svg)

Of the Singapore dollar's 49.5 per cent rise against the yen, 73 per cent was
the yen falling against everyone and 31 per cent was the Singapore dollar
rising against everyone. The remainder was 4 per cent the other way, so the
three add to the whole. The bet needed the yen's part to be at least half and
larger than the Singapore dollar's. It held. So did the first bet, at 55 per
cent: the split added up on both windows, with the ringgit's remainder at 17
per cent of the move, inside the line of 20 per cent, and the BIS rates within
a tenth of a per cent of MAS's own.

## Where I was wrong: the ringgit rose against everyone

**The ringgit bet failed, as I expected it would.** I gave it 8 per cent,
because the ringgit was strong in 2024 and 2025, and I expected it to end the
window higher against everyone than it started. It did. Over the window the
Singapore dollar rose only 5 per cent against the ringgit, while the ringgit
itself rose about 9 per cent against everyone. A currency that rises cannot
account for a fall. The Singapore dollar's own rise of 13 per cent was 254 per
cent of the move, about two and a half times the whole. The ringgit's rise
took back 171 per cent, and the remainder was 17 per cent. On the ringgit, the
Singapore dollar's own climb did the work. That is two sides to every rate:
against the yen, the yen moved most; against the ringgit, the Singapore
dollar.

**The tally.** 4 of 5 held against the 2.68 I expected. The Brier score, which
measures how far each confidence sat from what happened, was 0.136. A
forecaster who always says 50 per cent scores 0.25, and lower is better. I was
too unsure of the split closing and of the policy bet, on far too few bets to
judge calibration.

## The obvious objection: isn't steering just strength?

MAS's slope partly tracks the economy, so a path that follows MAS might be
following growth one step removed. The fifth bet tested that. Over the 61
stretches between MAS's decisions, the index's path and MAS's decisions
ranked those stretches in a partly similar order: a rank correlation of 0.47,
where 1 is the same order and 0 is no relation. With growth it was 0.11.
MAS's lead over growth was 0.35, against a pass line of 0.20; allowing for
chance, a 90 per cent range put it between 0.11 and 0.59.
The bet held, at 35 per cent.

## Where it flips: inflation, and two decades of the yen

Put CPI inflation in place of growth and the result turns over. The rank
correlation with inflation was 0.51, and MAS's lead over inflation was -0.04.
Inflation lined up with the path at least as well as MAS's decisions did. This
check was sealed as a sensitivity, and it is not scored.

It sits beside the sentence sealed with the bet, quoted here as written:

> The design leans toward the rival. The band is the path MAS keeps the index
> on, so a score that describes the band will match the index's path wherever
> MAS held the index inside it. A result that the path followed MAS's
> decisions is weak evidence that MAS rather than the economy moved the
> Singapore dollar. A result that it followed growth at least as closely is
> strong evidence against the rival.

MAS states its aim as price stability, so a path that matched inflation and
its decisions equally well is what its own account describes. The record
cannot separate the two.

The yen split turns over too, on the long window. From August 2005 the
Singapore dollar rose 87 per cent against the yen, and its own rise was 57 per
cent of the move against the yen's fall at 51 per cent. Over two decades the
Singapore dollar's own climb was the larger part; the yen's fall dominated
only the recent window, the one travellers remember. The long split was sealed
as context and does not change the verdict.

## The world view: steadier than a peg, steadier than all ten

![The typical monthly move of each currency's broad index since August 2005, eleven currencies ranked from steadiest to least steady](/figs/sgd_chart2_steady.svg)

Since August 2005 the Singapore dollar's broad index moved by about 0.47 per
cent in a typical month, the steadiest of the 11. The bet held at 80 per cent.
Hong Kong came second, at 1.00 per cent: its swing was about 0.78 of the US
dollar's, and the two broad indices moved together with a correlation of 0.92.
The passenger moved with the car, a little more gently. The
free-floating yen was the least steady of the 11, at 2.16 per cent.

## The MAS path: a slow line with turns at the decisions

The BIS index moved with MAS's own index month by month, with a correlation
of 0.92, so it describes the index MAS steers.

![The Singapore dollar's broad index since 2001, with each MAS decision marked beneath and GDP growth over each stretch between decisions](/figs/sgd_chart3_path.svg)

The index climbed in long, slow runs while MAS kept a rising slope. It went
flat or dipped in the stretches after MAS eased, in 2009 and 2020. Its two
steepest stretches came in 2022, when MAS kept the band rising and lifted the
whole band at once, the strongest setting on the scale the fifth bet used.
Not every stretch fitted: after the January 2015 decision the index fell while
the slope was still set to rise. That is the accounting the fifth bet scored:
what the path lined up with, not why MAS set the slopes it set.

## What would change my mind

- A Singapore dollar whose own rise against everyone outweighed the yen's fall
  over a later window.
- A broad index that swung like a floating currency's.
- A path that lined up with growth as closely as with MAS's decisions, which
  the design treats as strong evidence.

## The verdict: mostly the yen; the path lined up with MAS

Most of the Singapore dollar's rise against the yen was the yen falling against
everyone; most of its rise against the ringgit was the Singapore dollar rising
against everyone; its broad path followed MAS's decisions more closely than
growth.

Three parts are weaker than they read. Malaysia and Singapore sit heavily in
each other's baskets, so the ringgit result was sealed as weaker evidence than
the yen's. And the policy result was sealed as weak evidence, because the band
is drawn around the path; inflation matched the path as well. And the yen
result is about the recent window: since August 2005, the Singapore dollar's
own rise was the larger part.

## What this does not explain: prices, flows and motive

It covers exchange rates, not Japanese prices, which matter to a traveller as
much as the rate. It says nothing about interest rates or capital flows, or
about why MAS set the slopes it set. And the window was set by a rule, but I
knew the yen had fallen since 2021 when I wrote it; that was disclosed with
the sealed bets.
