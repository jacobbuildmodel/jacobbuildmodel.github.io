S$1,000 bought about 78,000 yen in January 2021 and about 120,000 in December
2025, at the Monetary Authority of Singapore's (MAS's) monthly average rates
of S$1.28 and S$0.83 per 100 yen. The yen got cheaper. Did Singapore get
richer? In Japan, a Singapore dollar bought about 32 per cent more of what
Japanese households buy, whichever currency moved (added after outside review, not
sealed). And mostly the yen moved: of the Singapore dollar's rise against the
yen, 73 per cent was the yen falling against Japan's trading partners and 31
per cent the Singapore dollar rising against Singapore's trading partners, with
4 per cent running the other way.

The 32 per cent and the shares use the tests' three-month averages at each
end; over those the rise was 49.5 per cent, and on the two single months above
it was 54 per cent. The shares are of the whole move, measured so that the
three add up exactly. Measured against the US dollar alone, the yen's share was
93 per cent; on every measure tried, the yen's fall was the larger part (added
after outside review, not sealed). This is an
accounting split of exchange rates: it says whose currency moved, and it does
not measure whether Singapore got richer (added after outside review, not
sealed).

Each currency's "everyone" in the bets below is its own index from the Bank
for International Settlements (BIS): its nominal value against the other
currencies in the BIS's broad set of 64 economies, weighted by trade in
manufactured goods, counting competition in third markets as well as direct
trade. The two currencies are measured against different baskets, with
different weights and each holding the other; that difference leaves the 4 per
cent remainder. The BIS
index is not MAS's own index, the S$NEER, but moved closely with it (added after outside
review, not sealed).

I sealed five bets on the split on an [open question page](/open/2026-10-03-singapore-dollar/) before
opening the data. Four held. The one I expected to fail, on the ringgit,
failed.

## The belief and the rival

The comfortable story says a strong economy makes a strong currency, so a
cheap yen is a sign of Singapore's strength. The rival says that since 1981
MAS has steered the Singapore dollar against a basket of trading partners'
currencies, inside an unpublished band that, its framework FAQ says, crawls
"at different rates over time, depending on current and future expected
price developments in the economy", so most of a move against one currency
is that currency moving.

## My bets: five predictions, sealed before the data

Each currency's broad index is the BIS index defined above (added after
outside review, not sealed).

1. **The split adds up** (55 per cent): the remainder within 20 per cent of the
   move on both windows, 2021-25 and August 2005 on (after the ringgit's and
   the renminbi's pegs to the US dollar ended in July 2005), and BIS rates
   within half a per cent of MAS's.
2. **The yen's share** (90 per cent): at least half the rise was the yen's
   fall, and more than the Singapore dollar's own rise.
3. **The ringgit's share** (8 per cent): the same, for the ringgit.
4. **The slow path** (80 per cent): the steadiest or second steadiest broad
   index of 11: the Singapore dollar, yen, ringgit, won, renminbi, baht,
   rupiah, US dollar, euro, Australian dollar and Hong Kong dollar.
5. **Policy, not growth** (35 per cent): its path lined up clearly better with
   MAS's decisions than with growth.

## What the data said: nearly three-quarters was the yen's fall

![For the yen and the ringgit, three moves in per cent from early 2021 to late 2025: the Singapore dollar against everyone, the partner against everyone, and the Singapore dollar against the partner](/figs/sgd_chart1_split.svg)

The Singapore dollar's broad index rose 13 per cent; the yen's broad index fell
25 per cent. Of the 49.5 per cent rise against the yen, 73 per cent was the
yen's fall and 31 per cent the Singapore dollar's rise, with 4 per cent the
other way; as log shares, the three add up exactly. The bet held. So did the
first: the remainder stayed within 20 per cent of the move on both windows,
and BIS rates came within half a per cent of MAS's.

Against the US dollar instead, the yen's share was even larger, 93 per cent,
while the ringgit's split depended on what it was measured against (added
after outside review, not sealed).

## In most five-year stretches the yen moved more; two decades cannot be ranked

![Every five-year window since August 2005: the Singapore dollar's rise and the yen's fall as shares of the Singapore dollar's rise against the yen, with the windows where it did not rise shaded](/figs/sgd_chart4_rolling.svg)

In most five-year windows (August 2005 on, three months apart; only four fit
end to end without overlapping) where the
Singapore dollar gained on the yen, the yen's fall was the larger part.
Consecutive windows share 57 of 60 months, so those windows are the same
stretches of yen weakness counted many times over. A currency kept on a
gentle slope, beside one that floats, would tend to give that result; that is
reasoning, not a finding here (all added after outside review, not sealed).
Over the whole span the Singapore dollar rose 87 per cent against the yen:
its rise against everyone 57 per cent of the move, the yen's fall against
everyone 51, a remainder of -8, sealed as context. The two decades cannot be
ranked: the remainder, 8 points in size, is larger than the gap between 57
and 51, and against the US dollar alone the order reverses (added after
outside review, not sealed).

## What failed, as I expected: the ringgit rose against everyone

I gave the ringgit bet 8 per cent, expecting the strong ringgit of 2024 and
2025 to end the window higher against everyone. It did. Both currencies rose
against the world, and the Singapore dollar rose a little more. That small gap
is most of the Singapore dollar's 5 per cent rise against the ringgit. That is
two sides to every rate: against the yen, the yen
moved most; against the ringgit, the Singapore dollar.

**The tally.** 4 of 5 held against the 2.68 I expected, the five confidences
added up. What is scored is each confidence, not the hit rate: the Brier score,
how far each sat from what happened, was 0.136, against 0.25 for always saying
50 per cent (lower is better). Saying 8 per cent and seeing that bet fail
counted in my favour.

## The obvious objection: isn't steering just strength?

MAS's slope partly tracks the economy, so a path that follows MAS might
follow growth one step removed. Over the 61 stretches between decisions, the
path's rank correlation with MAS's decisions was 0.47 (1 is the same order, 0
no relation), and with growth 0.11. MAS's lead, 0.35, beat the pass line of
0.20, but its 90 per cent range, 0.11 to 0.59, reaches below that line, so the
lead is not clearly above it. The bet held; I had given it 35 per cent. A finer
coding of the slope gave a lead of 0.346.

On monthly changes, the BIS index and MAS's own index moved together with a
correlation of 0.92, so the BIS index tracked the one MAS steers.

![The Singapore dollar's broad index since 2001, with each MAS decision marked beneath and GDP growth over each stretch between decisions](/figs/sgd_chart3_path.svg)

## Where it flips: inflation lined up as well as MAS did

With CPI inflation in place of growth the result turns over: a rank correlation
of 0.51, and MAS's lead -0.04, a sealed sensitivity, not scored. Beside it sits
the sentence sealed with the bet:

> The design leans toward the rival. The band is the path MAS keeps the index
> on, so a score that describes the band will match the index's path wherever
> MAS held the index inside it. A result that the path followed MAS's
> decisions is weak evidence that MAS rather than the economy moved the
> Singapore dollar. A result that it followed growth at least as closely is
> strong evidence against the rival.

## The world view: steadier even than Hong Kong's dollar

![The typical monthly move of each currency's broad index since August 2005, eleven currencies ranked from steadiest to least steady](/figs/sgd_chart2_steady.svg)

Since August 2005 the Singapore dollar's broad index moved about 0.47 per cent
in a typical month (the standard deviation of the monthly change), the
steadiest of the 11; the bet held. Hong Kong's came second, at 1.00 per cent,
by design: its dollar is pegged to the US dollar alone, so its value against a
basket swings with the US dollar's. The yen was the least steady, at 2.16 per
cent.

## What would change my mind

- A later five-year window where the Singapore dollar's own rise outweighed
  the yen's fall.
- A broad index that swung like a floating currency's.
- A path that lined up with growth as closely as with MAS's decisions.

## The verdict: mostly the yen; the path lined up with MAS

Most of the Singapore dollar's rise against the yen was the yen falling against
everyone; most of its rise against the ringgit was the Singapore dollar rising
against everyone; its broad path followed MAS's decisions more closely than
growth.

With CPI inflation in place of growth, MAS's lead was -0.04, so the record
cannot separate MAS's decisions from inflation, which MAS's policy aims to keep
low and stable. With inflation a quarter ahead it was -0.01 (added after outside
review, not sealed).

Weaker than they read:

- **The ringgit:** the two countries weigh heavily in each other's baskets,
  so it was sealed as weaker evidence.
- **The policy result:** sealed as weak evidence; the band is drawn around
  the path.
- **The yen:** true of the recent window on every measure tried; over two
  decades the two parts cannot be ranked (added after outside review, not
  sealed).

## What it changes: in 2021-25 the yen fell more than the Singapore dollar rose

The evidence cut against reading the cheap yen of 2021-25 as a sign of
Singapore's strength: the yen's fall against its trading partners was the
larger part, as it was in most five-year stretches when the Singapore dollar
gained on the yen. Over two decades, the yen's fall and the Singapore
dollar's rise cannot be ranked against each other; in Japan a Singapore dollar
bought more whichever currency moved (added after outside review, not sealed).

## What this does not explain: prices, flows and motive

The 32 per cent above is the 49.5 per cent more yen adjusted for Japan's
consumer prices, which rose 13 per cent (Japan's national index, not Tokyo's). The
real exchange rate rose more, 54.8 per cent, because Singapore's prices rose
faster, 17 per cent. Split the same way, with a remainder that takes up the
gap between this bilateral rate and the BIS's two broad real indices, the yen's
real fall against its trading partners was still 71 per cent of that real rise
and the Singapore dollar's real rise 31 per cent, with 2 per cent running the
other way, measured so the three add up (all added after outside review, not
sealed). Nothing here tests interest rates, capital flows or MAS's motives. I
knew the yen had fallen since 2021 when the window's rule was set, and
disclosed it with the sealed bets.
