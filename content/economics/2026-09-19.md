Is an HDB flat harder to afford than it used to be? Affordability is measured here as a ratio:
the price of a flat over what a household earns in a year. From 2013 to 2025 the price of a constant-quality four-room resale flat rose between 35.6 and 40.4 per cent, depending on how quality is held constant, and median household income from work rose 51.3 per cent, so the flat went from 5.43 years of income to between 4.86 and 5.04. The base year decides the sign: from the trough of 2019, at about 4.0 years, the same measure rose. The HDB loan rate did not
move over the period. Grants rose and the loan-to-value cap was cut three times, and for this
project's buyer at the 20th percentile of income a larger grant stopped lowering the monthly
repayment once that cap set the size of the loan.

*Corrected on 20 November 2026:* the grant tests now run from 2013, and method B is built as the seal describes it; the full note is in the method block below and on the corrections page.

## The measure and the levers: a ratio of price to income, grants, and a cap on the loan

Two instruments bear on what a buyer must find. Housing grants, means-tested, lower what a
first-time buyer has to borrow; the loan-to-value cap limits how much of the price may be
borrowed at all. In this project's calculation the loan is the smaller of the two: the cap, or the
price less grants. A
comparison of prices alone leaves out both the income the price is paid from and which of the two
limits sets the loan.

## The base year: 2019 was the cheapest year in more than a decade

Ask when flats became unaffordable and you will be told a story about the last five
years. It is a true story. The Resale Price Index bottomed in the second quarter of
2019 and has climbed by more than half since. Anyone who started looking in 2020
watched the thing they wanted move away from them.

But 2019 was the cheapest year in more than a decade, and choosing it as the
starting point decides the answer before any arithmetic happens. Prices had been
falling since 2013. A window that opens at a trough and closes near a peak shows a
sharp worsening whatever else is true.

So this piece runs from 2013, which was a peak, to 2025, the last complete year, and
measures one thing: the price of a constant-quality four-room resale flat against
what households earn. Because the same flat in different years is not the same thing
as whatever flats happened to sell that year, the composition of sales is corrected
for twice, by two methods that assume different things.

## Prices: a constant-quality four-room flat rose between 36 and 40 per cent from 2013 to 2025

Between 2013 and 2025, a constant-quality four-room resale flat rose between **35.6 and 40.4 per cent** in price. The upper figure is the year-on-year path from a hedonic regression on 121,765 four-room transactions, holding town, floor area, storey height, flat model and remaining lease still (method A). The lower figure is a fixed basket built as sealed, which assumes nothing about functional form: the median price in each cell of town and floor-area band, weighted by each cell's share of 2017 sales, a Laspeyres index; cells with fewer than five sales in any year are left out (method B). The uncorrected median gives 38.2 per cent, between the two.

The two corrected figures are 4.8 percentage points apart. I pre-registered that they would agree within 2 points, and that if they differed by more than 5, no single constant-quality number would be published and a range would be published instead. They land between those lines, so both are published here, and the composition correction is doing contested work: method A puts the constant-quality rise above the raw median, method B below it. The gap is also fragile. Of eight other choices of floor-area bands and minimum sales per cell, tried after the result, seven put it past 5 points; across all nine it runs from 4.5 to 6.0. The first version reported 37.9 per cent for the basket, from cells that were also split by remaining lease and weighted by 2013 sales, which is not the method as sealed.

## Incomes: median household income rose 51.3 per cent, so the ratio fell

Over the same period, median monthly household income from work, excluding the
employer's CPF contribution, rose **51.3 per cent**. That is the basis HDB itself
assesses income on, which is why it is the one used here.

Thirty-six to forty against fifty-one. The ratio fell on either method.

A four-room flat cost **5.43 years** of median annual household income in 2013. It cost **4.86 to 5.04 years** in 2025. In between it fell to **4.04 years** in 2019 (4.05 on method B), which was the cheapest it has been in the whole window on either method.

Run the same measure from 2017 and it rises by 0.54 to 0.76 years, which is a clear worsening. Run it from 2013 and it falls by 0.39 to 0.56 years. Same data, same method, same
flat. The base year decides the sign.

## Against 2019: about 4 years of income then, 4.9 to 5.0 in 2025

Because almost nobody is comparing today with 2013. They are comparing it with
2019, or with what a cousin paid in 2019, and against that year the deterioration is
real and large. The 2019 buyer got the best deal available in twelve years. That is
a fact about 2019, not a fact about now.

There is also a second thing happening, and it is not visible in the price at all.

## Grants: the grant rose, and the loan-to-value cap took over the loan

Singapore is unusual in that the cost of borrowing has not moved. The HDB
concessionary loan rate is pegged at 0.1 percentage points above the CPF Ordinary
Account rate, and the Ordinary Account has sat at its legislated 2.5 per cent floor
throughout. CPF announcements ten years apart, for the first quarter of 2016 and the
third quarter of 2026, both state the same two numbers: 2.5 per cent, and an HDB
rate of 2.6 per cent.

Elsewhere, falling mortgage rates have repeatedly rescued affordability measures
while prices climbed. Here that rescue was never available. Which leaves grants as
the only lever that moved, and grants are means-tested.

For a buyer at the 20th percentile of household income, total grants rose from 50,000 dollars in 2013 to 145,000 in 2025. Over the same period the price of the flat rose by 184,157 dollars on the hedonic path; the grant and repayment figures here use that path, with method B's in brackets. The grant increase covered **51.6 per cent** of the price increase (58.6), more than the 40 per cent I pre-registered. Measured from 2019 alone, when the grant was 105,000 dollars and prices were near their low, it covered 17 per cent (18.7).

For that buyer the mortgage servicing ratio, the share of monthly income going to the loan, fell from 60.4 per cent in 2013 to 47.1 per cent in 2025 (45.4). For the median buyer it fell 6.0 points (6.7), and for a buyer at the 80th percentile 3.0 points (3.4). Measured from 2019 instead, all three rose: the 20th-percentile buyer's from 37.8 to 47.1 per cent, the median buyer's by 1.8 points and the 80th percentile's by 0.6. The 20th-percentile buyer's shares are above HDB's limit on a loan's monthly repayment: 30 per cent of gross monthly income since 27 August 2013, 35 per cent before. The measure here applies the loan-to-value cap but not that limit, so it shows what this flat costs a buyer on this income, not a loan HDB would grant.

The pre-registered test said the low-income buyer's change would be at least 5 points smaller than the high-income buyer's, because that is what means-testing is for. Measured from 2013, it is 10.3 points smaller (11.5): both fell, the low-income buyer's further. That meets the line written for this prediction to hold. It also meets a second line in the same seal, for burdens that "move in the same direction but differ by more than 5 percentage points", which the seal says is reported as "heterogeneity without sign divergence". A result that meets both lines is recorded as neither. Almost all of the gap reflects where each buyer started, not grants (see the note on comparing the three buyers). The second test, that the low-income buyer's repayment share would be no higher in 2025 than in 2013, also passed: it fell from 60.4 to 47.1 per cent (45.4). The grants did not do that. By 2025 the loan-to-value cap set that buyer's loan, so with the grant held at its 2013 level the share would still have been 47.1; it fell because that buyer's income rose faster than the loan (51.7 against 18.3 per cent; 14.2 on method B). What happened between 2019 and 2025 is still the most interesting thing here: over those years the grant rose and the low-income buyer's repayment rose too.

![Which constraint sets the loan](/figs/fig3_binding_constraint.svg)

*Figure 3. Grants set the loan in 2019. The loan-to-value cap sets it by 2025. For a
buyer at the 20th percentile the loan is the lower of the two lines, and they swap
over, which is why a larger grant stopped reducing the monthly repayment.*

The loan is the smaller of two things: the loan-to-value cap, and the price less
whatever grants the buyer receives. In 2013 and in 2019 the cap was 90 per cent of the price, and for a low-income buyer with a large grant the second number was smaller. The grant
set the size of the loan directly.

The cap was then cut three times, to 85 per cent in December 2021, 80 in September
2022, and 75 in August 2024. By 2025 the cap is the binding constraint. An extra
dollar of grant no longer reduces the loan at all. It reduces the deposit instead,
and for that buyer grants had covered the deposit in full until 2024. The grant went up
and the thing it was doing stopped.

This is a correlation between two policy changes and one outcome, not a causal
estimate. Both levers moved at once and nothing here separates them.

## The grant ceiling: median income passed 9,000 dollars between 2022 and 2023

The Enhanced CPF Housing Grant carries a household income ceiling of 9,000 dollars a
month. It was set at 9,000 when the grant was introduced in September 2019 and it is
still 9,000 today. Over those seven years the maximum grant rose from 80,000 dollars
to 120,000, and the separate ceiling governing flat purchase and the CPF Housing
Grant moved from 12,000 to 14,000 and then to 16,000.

Median household income crossed 9,000 between **2022 and 2023**: 8,904 dollars a month
in 2022, 9,646 in 2023.

![Median income against the frozen grant ceiling](/figs/fig2_ehg_crossing.svg)

*Figure 2. Median household income passed the Enhanced CPF Housing Grant ceiling
between the 2022 and 2023 readings (2022.1 on a straight line between them). The
ceiling has been 9,000 dollars a month since the grant was introduced in September
2019. Income here is an upper bound on HDB's assessed basis, which excludes bonuses,
so on HDB's basis the crossing is no earlier.*

In dollars, the median household's Enhanced CPF Housing Grant went from 10,000 in
2019 to zero from 2023. A threshold that does not move admits fewer households every
year, and nobody has to change anything for that to happen.

The other half of that sentence matters just as much. The median household's **total**
grant still rose, from 60,000 dollars to 80,000, because the CPF Housing Grant for
resale flats went up by 30,000 at Budget 2023 and that grant is not tapered by income.
The middle lost the means-tested grant and gained more from the flat one. Quoting
either half alone would mislead.

## Where it could move next: the gap between income and price, the grant ceiling, the binding constraint

Watch the gap between income growth and price growth, not the price. Prices rose 36 to 40 per cent over twelve years and the ratio still fell, because incomes rose 51 per cent. If income growth slows while prices hold, the ratio worsens with no headline
attached to it.

Watch the Enhanced CPF Housing Grant ceiling. It has been 9,000 dollars since 2019
while every other threshold in the system has moved. If it stays there, the share of
households receiving any means-tested housing grant keeps falling on its own. If it
moves, that is a policy change large enough to redo this analysis for.

And watch which constraint binds. A larger grant does nothing for a monthly repayment
once the loan-to-value cap is the thing setting the loan. That is not visible in the
grant amount, which is the number that gets announced.

**Tested here:** whether a constant-quality four-room resale flat took more years of median
household income in 2025 than in 2013 (fewer: 4.86 to 5.04 against 5.43), how far that answer depends on
the base year and on the method of holding quality constant, and how the repayment burden of
buyers at three levels of income changed as grants rose and the loan-to-value cap was cut.

**Not tested here:** why prices moved. Among the standard candidates are the cooling measures of
[December 2021](https://www.mof.gov.sg/news-resources/newsroom/measures-to-cool-the-property-market/) and [September 2022](https://www.mas.gov.sg/news/media-releases/2022/measures-to-promote-sustainable-conditions-in-the-property-market-by-ensuring-prudent-borrowing-and-moderating-demand) and the building delays of
the pandemic ([HDB, annual report 2022/23](https://www.hdb.gov.sg/-/media/hdb-pulse/reports/annual-reports-and-financial-statements/HDB-AR-FY22.pdf)). All of them fall inside the window, and this project does not
separate them.

### Three ways this could be wrong: the base year, the composition method, the income series

The 2013 starting point would have to be the wrong peak. If the right comparison is
with some earlier year when the ratio was lower than 5.43, the fall I report is an
artefact of where I started, exactly as the 2017 comparison is. The transaction data
is on a consistent registration-date basis only from March 2012, so I cannot test
that here.

The two composition methods would have to diverge further. Built as sealed, they differ by 4.8 percentage points, between my pre-registered lines for agreement and for failure, and most other choices of cells, tried after the result, put the gap past the 5-point line. If a third method landed well outside both, the range would widen.

SingStat's income series would have to be the wrong denominator. It covers resident
employed households, not flat buyers, who are younger and differently distributed.
It also includes one-twelfth of the annual bonus, and HDB's assessment excludes
bonuses, so it sits above the basis HDB actually uses. That is why the 2022 crossing
is stated as a bound rather than a date.

### Not explained here: why prices moved, grants from 2014 to 2018, other buyers, bank loans

Why prices moved. Two rounds of cooling measures, a fifteen-month wait-out rule for
private owners and its removal, pandemic construction delays and the BTO supply cycle
all sit inside this window. They are confounds, they are not separated here, and this
piece makes no claim about what caused the price path.

Grants from 2014 to 2018. The Enhanced CPF Housing Grant replaced the Additional CPF Housing Grant in September 2019. The older grant's schedule, with an income ceiling of 5,000 dollars a month and a maximum of 40,000, dates from HDB's release of 6 February 2009 and was still in force in 2013, so the grant tests compare 2013 with 2025, as sealed; the first version ran them from 2019 (see the correction). The grants of the years between are not modelled, so Figure 3 starts in 2019.

Anyone who is not a first-timer family. Second-timers, singles and non-citizen
households face different grants or none. The Proximity Housing Grant is excluded
throughout because it depends on living near a parent rather than on income.

Bank-loan borrowers. Everything here assumes an HDB concessionary loan at 2.6 per
cent. A buyer on a bank loan faced a very different rate path after 2022.

### Notes on the composition correction and on comparing the three buyers

#### Why the hedonic correction matters less than expected

The mix of flats sold changes every year. More Sengkang and Punggol, fewer mature
estates; newer leases, then ageing ones. A rising median price is partly a changing
basket rather than a rising price.

Restricting the whole analysis to four-room flats removes most of that drift before
any modelling starts, which is why the hedonic and uncorrected numbers end up only 2.2 percentage points apart. The pre-registered threshold for "composition mattered" was 3 points, so on the letter of the test it did not, quite. Method B, built as sealed, is 2.6 points from the uncorrected median, and on the other side of it: the hedonic index rose more than the raw median, method B less. The scorecard scores this test on the hedonic index.

The hedonic was also refitted with remaining lease as a piecewise-linear spline
rather than the pre-registered quadratic, because the quadratic came back with the
wrong curvature. The spline recovers the expected shape, where value decays much
faster below sixty years of remaining lease, and moves the hedonic figure from 40.4 to 40.6 per cent. The misspecification was real and the index was insulated from it.

#### A caution about comparing the three buyers

Measured from 2013, the 20th-percentile buyer starts at a mortgage servicing ratio of 60.4 per cent and the 80th-percentile buyer at 13.8. The same proportional change therefore produces a much larger change in percentage points for the poorer buyer. In proportional terms the three buyers moved almost together: down 22.0, 22.7 and 22.0 per cent for the 20th, 50th and 80th percentiles (24.7, 25.3 and 24.7 on method B). So almost all of the 10.3-point gap in the pre-registered test reflects where each buyer started. From 2019 the proportional changes were up 24.7, 9.7 and 5.4 per cent.

The 2019 figures are in the repository. The 2013 figures were recomputed for the correction of 20 November 2026 from the repository's committed price, income, loan-to-value and rate series, with the 2013 grants added; the repository's grant table and method B will be updated to match.
