> Two bidding exercises run every month, about a fortnight apart. If you have any
> flexibility about which one you bid in, the choice is worth roughly 1% of the premium.
> Knowing both prices in advance, which nobody does, would be worth about S$1,840.

The [previous piece](/economics/2026-08-22/) argued that the quota is a weak lever on COE
prices. This one asks the smaller question a person actually faces, which is whether the
timing of a bid matters at all.

It is a good question to ask of this data because it can be answered cleanly. Both exercises
sit inside the same calendar month, so incomes, credit conditions, the year's quota
allocation and everything else that moves slowly are the same for both. Comparing them
requires no controls, and adding any would be dishonest.

Bidding happens twice a month, and the two exercises do not clear at the same price. Anyone who
has waited a fortnight for the second round has been told it is the cheaper one, or the dearer
one, depending on who is telling them.

There is a real pattern in the file. The question is whether it is large enough to act on,
which is a different question from whether it exists.

What follows is the size of the gap, what perfect timing would have been worth, and how that
compares with the variation you cannot predict.

## Two exercises a month, and one is dearer

Across 196 complete months from January 2010 to July 2026:

| Category | Average gap | p | Second exercise cheaper |
|---|---|---|---|
| A, smaller cars | +0.61% | 0.18 | 44% of months |
| B, larger cars | **+1.31%** | **0.003** | 43% |
| C, goods vehicles | +0.12% | 0.79 | 46% |
| D, motorcycles | -0.07% | 0.92 | 49% |
| E, open | **+1.11%** | **0.004** | 38% |

A positive number means the second exercise of the month clears higher.

Categories B and E survive a correction for having run five tests at once. They are not two
separate confirmations, though. Open-category certificates get bid up for large cars, so B
and E are linked by arbitrage, and their monthly gaps correlate at 0.81. It is one finding
appearing twice.

## It is a real round effect, not a rising market

There is an obvious way this could be an illusion. In a market drifting upward, the second
exercise is dearer for no reason except that it happens later.

The test is to split the month's drift into two fortnights: the one inside the month, and
the one that crosses into the next month. Under pure drift those are equal.

| Category | Inside the month | Across the boundary | Difference | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | +0.63% | +0.37% | +0.25% | 0.74 |
| B | +1.32% | -0.33% | **+1.65%** | **0.024** |
| C | +0.13% | +0.70% | -0.57% | 0.35 |
| D | -0.07% | +1.33% | -1.38% | 0.24 |
| E | +1.11% | -0.14% | **+1.26%** | **0.002** |

For B and E the rise inside the month is larger than the rise across the boundary, so
something about being the second exercise is doing work. For A, C and D there is nothing to
explain.

## The pattern that looks predictable is arithmetic

A buyer sees the first exercise clear before the second one opens, so the interesting
question is whether that tells you anything.

It looks like it does. Regress the within-month gap on how far the price jumped going into
the first exercise, and Category A gives a coefficient of -0.32 with a p-value of 0.0002. A
big jump into the first exercise predicts a fall in the second. That is a strong-looking
result and I believed it for about a day.

It is arithmetic. The predictor contains the first exercise's price and the outcome
subtracts it, so the same number appears on both sides with opposite signs. Any random
wobble in a single exercise therefore produces a negative coefficient with no predictability
behind it.

Simulating markets that contain no exploitable pattern at all, 2,000 runs each:

| Simulated market | Coefficient |
|---|---|
| Random walk, no transitory noise | -0.001 |
| Random walk plus transitory noise, half variance | -0.167 |
| Random walk plus transitory noise, equal variance | **-0.333** |

The observed -0.32 sits inside the range a market with nothing in it produces. The one
predictor that shares no term with the outcome, last month's gap, is insignificant for
Category A at p = 0.12.

Fitting the forecasting rule on 2010 to 2018 and running it month by month from 2019 settles
it. For Category A it picks the cheaper exercise 52.3% of the time. Two of the five
categories come out nominally significant, and they point in opposite directions: Category D
is significantly worse than a coin, Category E significantly better. That is what five
simultaneous tests look like when none of them is real.

## A rule fixed in advance did save money

There is one rule that needs no forecast. Pick a side once, on the evidence available in
2018, and never revisit it.

| Category | Gap to 2018 | Rule | Result 2019 to 2026 | Per purchase |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | +0.33% | first exercise | -0.55% | **+$402** |
| B | +0.91% | first exercise | -0.83% | **+$733** |
| C | -0.42% | second exercise | +0.38% | -$214 |
| D | -0.21% | second exercise | +0.09% | -$8 |
| E | +0.78% | first exercise | -0.69% | **+$618** |

It worked out of sample in the three car categories and failed in the two where the
pre-2019 sign was noise to begin with.

Before treating that as advice, look at what the number sits inside.

{{< figure src="/figs/gap_distribution.svg" alt="Histogram of the gap between the two Category A bidding exercises of the same month, across 196 months. The mean is plus 0.61 percent and the standard deviation is 7.2 percent, so the average is far smaller than the spread." caption="Figure 1. The within-month gap, Category A. The mean is real and it is small next to the spread." >}}

The average gap is +0.61% and the spread around it is 7.2%. In any single month the rule is
close to a coin flip. It shows up across many purchases, and a person buys a car every
several years.

## What it is worth against what you cannot control

Three numbers put the size of this in perspective.

**The mean gap between the month's two exercises is 5.1%, ignoring direction. The mean change
over six months is 15.2%.** Waiting half a year moves the price three times as much as
choosing the exercise, and the previous piece showed that direction is not forecastable
either.

**Perfect foresight is worth 2.25%.** If you knew both prices before choosing, every month,
you would save about $1,840 on a recent Category A certificate. That is the ceiling on any
within-month timing skill that could ever exist, and nobody is near it.

**In the recent era the gap in dollars is about $1,161 for Category A**, on an average
premium of $98,838 in the first exercise and $99,999 in the second.

## What would prove this wrong

**The sign reverses for six years.** Through 2014 to 2019 every category had a negative
average gap, meaning the second exercise was the cheaper one. That is a third of the sample
and the only stretch when premiums were falling. A pattern that inverts for six years is not
a law.

**Category B loses significance if you drop its strongest era.** Excluding 2010 to 2013
takes it from +1.31% to +0.69% at p = 0.14. The split-half check is kinder, holding in both
halves of the sample, but the result leans on particular periods more than the headline
number suggests.

**B and E are one finding.** Their gaps correlate at 0.81, so treating them as two
independent confirmations overstates the evidence.

**196 months is not many** for an effect of 1% against a spread of 7%.

**None of this says anything about which month or which year to buy in.** It compares two
exercises a fortnight apart. The larger swings, the ones that actually determine what a
certificate costs, are the subject of the other piece, and the answer there is that they are
not forecastable from this data either.

What the bidding record supports is narrow: across many purchases the first exercise of the
month has tended to clear slightly lower in the car categories, the tendency reversed for six
years in the middle of the sample, and the most that perfect timing could ever be worth
inside a month is about 2%. Everything larger than that is outside the reach of this data.

## What to take from this

The round effect is real and it is small. Perfect timing, every single exercise, across the
whole period, would have been worth about $1,840. That is a genuine number and it sits inside
noise several times larger.

The useful distinction is between a pattern that exists and a pattern you can trade. This one
exists. It is swamped by variation nobody can forecast, which is why the people who swear the
second round is cheaper and the people who swear it is dearer can both point at exercises that
prove them right.

What did work was a rule fixed in advance and followed regardless. Not because the rule
predicted anything, but because it removed the temptation to wait for a better week, and
waiting is what actually costs money in a market that trends upward.

If you are bidding, the honest guidance is that timing within the month is close to a rounding
error against the decision of whether to bid at all this year.
