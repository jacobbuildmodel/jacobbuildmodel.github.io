> Motorcycle premiums peaked in 2022, averaging $10,945. Across the 39 bidding exercises
> since the start of 2025 they have averaged $9,133, a fall of 17%. Category A car premiums
> rose 43% over the same stretch. Same auction, same rules, opposite direction.

COE prices are a one-way story in most conversations here. They go up, they set records, and
the only argument is about whose fault it is.

One category has spent four years going the other way. Motorcycle premiums peaked in 2022 and
have been falling since, while every other category sits close to its own record. That is
unusual enough to be worth explaining rather than noting.

What follows is the size of the divergence, the one thing that separates motorcycles from
everything else, and a category that does not fit the explanation.

## Everyone assumes COE prices only go one way

The belief is that COE premiums are a ratchet. They pause sometimes, and then they resume
climbing, and issuing more certificates does not change that.

It is a fair reading of the last few years. Category A, Category B, Category C and the Open
category are all within 2% of their own record right now. An [earlier piece on this
site](/economics/2026-08-22/) found that a 62% rise in the car quota since 2010 should have
pushed premiums down about 14%, and they rose 287% instead. Someone who read that could
reasonably conclude the quota lever does not work.

One category disagrees.

## One category went the other way

Every comparison in this piece runs from 2022, the year motorcycle premiums peaked, to the
39 bidding exercises held since the start of 2025. Pooling 2025 and 2026 avoids resting a
ratio on the 15 exercises that 2026 has run so far.

Category D is a noisy series, so the gap needs checking rather than asserting. Comparing the
24 exercises in 2022 against the 39 exercises in 2025 and 2026 gives a difference of $1,812
with a p-value below 0.0001. It is not noise.

Every other category is at or within 2% of its peak. Motorcycles are 17% below theirs.

The endpoint matters less than it might. Against full-year 2025 alone the motorcycle fall is
18% and the car rise 36%. Against the part year of 2026 it is 14% and 55%. The pooled figures
sit between the two, and the part year happens to understate the motorcycle fall while
overstating the car rise, so the headline is the conservative version.

## Nobody new turned up to bid

Here is what changed in each category, 2022 against the exercises since the start of 2025.

| Category | Quota | Bids received | Premium |
|---|---|---|---|
| A, smaller cars | +125% | +149% | +43% |
| B, larger cars | +59% | +67% | +20% |
| C, goods vehicles | +221% | +139% | +29% |
| **D, motorcycles** | **+10%** | **-1%** | **-17%** |
| E, open | +39% | +58% | +19% |

Motorcycles are the only category where the number of bids did not grow. They are also the
only category where the premium fell. Everywhere else bidding rose by between 58% and 149%,
and the price rose with it.

Motorcycle bidding has sat between about 620 and 710 bids per exercise every year since 2021.
Car bidding roughly doubled over the same stretch. The motorcycle quota rose 10%, which is
modest, and it was enough, because nothing was pushing back against it.

There is a second thing working in the same direction. Motorcycles respond more to a change
in supply than any other category. The earlier piece estimated how much each premium moves
when the quota moves, and for motorcycles the figure is more than twice the car one. Put
another way, cutting the motorcycle premium by 10% takes about 16% more certificates. Doing
the same for Category A cars takes about 41%.

So motorcycles had both things going for them. A category that reacts strongly to supply, and
no growth in the number of people bidding.

That is the useful correction to the earlier piece. **The quota is not a weak lever
everywhere. It was a weak lever for cars because demand kept outrunning it.**

## What would prove this wrong

**Category C already complicates it.** Its quota rose 221% against bids up 139%, so supply
grew faster than bidding, and the premium still rose 29%. If the relationship were mechanical
that could not happen. It is not mechanical.

I also tested the obvious tidy version of this story, that the premium simply tracks how many
people chase each certificate. It does not survive. Measured across all 393 exercises the
correlation between bids per certificate and the premium is under 0.16 in every category, and
in first differences it works for cars and is indistinguishable from zero for goods vehicles
and the open category. A neat mechanism would have been better. This data does not support
one.

So what is left is a description of what happened in one category, not a law. If motorcycle
bidding stayed flat for another three years while premiums climbed back to $11,000, this
piece would be wrong.

## What this does not explain

**Why motorcycle bidding stopped growing.** This is the question that matters and the bidding
file cannot answer it. Delivery riding, licensing rules, the price of the bike itself and
whatever else moves a person to own one are all outside this data.

**Whether $9,133 is affordable.** A motorcycle COE has still gone from $1,309 in 2010 to
about $9,100 now, a rise of nearly 600%, the largest of any category over the full period.
Falling 17% from a peak is not the same as being cheap.

**Anything about individual buyers.** These are bids, not bidders, and a bid is often placed
by a dealer.

## What to take from this

Motorcycles are the exception that shows what drives the rest. Premiums there have fallen
because bidding stopped growing, and bidding stopped growing while every other category kept
attracting more bidders each year.

That is the same finding as the quota piece from the other direction. Prices in this market
track how many people want in, not how many certificates exist. Where the crowd stopped
growing, the price stopped rising, without any policy change doing the work.

Category C is the honest complication. Its quota grew faster than its bidding and the premium
still rose, which means the story is not complete and the piece says so rather than trimming
the counter-example.

The thing to watch is bids per exercise rather than the premium. It moves first, and in
motorcycles it moved years before the price followed.
