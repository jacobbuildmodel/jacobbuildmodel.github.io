> **Verdict:** the record cannot tell COE and ERP apart.\
> **Key number:** from 2005 to 2017, cars per lane of road rose by 22 to 25 per cent; peak-hour speeds barely moved.\
> **My confidence going in:** 1.9 of 4 predictions expected to hold.\
> **Scored:** 1 of 4 held.\
> **What would change it:** a year-by-year record of how hard ERP was charging, or several years of peak speeds below LTA's target band.

Singapore charges for a car twice. The Certificate of Entitlement charges for
owning one, once. Electronic Road Pricing charges for using a busy road at a busy
hour, every time. Both are said to keep traffic moving. In September I wrote down
four bets on what the record would show, with how sure I was of each, and put
them on an [open question page](/open/2026-09-26-coe-or-erp/) before looking at the
data. One held. The roads got more crowded and stayed about as fast, which is
what ERP promises, but the record cannot say whether ERP is the reason.

The yardstick throughout is weekday peak-hour road speed, as the Land Transport
Authority (LTA) publishes it.

## Each side has a strong case

**For COE.** A car that is never bought is never driven, parked or left idling at
a junction. The quota is a hard ceiling fixed in advance, so car numbers cannot
run away, and it deals with every cost of a car at once, not only congestion.

**For ERP.** Congestion is about how many cars are in one place at one time. A
charge on the jammed road at the jammed hour lands where the harm is done, and it
corrects itself: LTA raises it when speeds fall below a target band and cuts it
when they rise above. A fee paid once changes behaviour once. A charge on every
trip changes it every morning.

## Theory leans one way, then hedges

In 1920 Arthur Pigou argued that the right charge for anything that harms other
people is the size of the harm. That is what ERP tries to be. William Vickrey
later worked out why the charge belongs on the peak, and Singapore's 1975 Area
Licensing Scheme was the first in the world to charge for entering a congested
city centre. In 1974 Martin Weitzman asked when a cap on quantity beats a price:
when harm climbs steeply past some point, a cap is safer, because a price set
slightly wrong lets the harm run away. Congestion is that kind of harm, which on
paper favours COE. But ERP is not a fixed price. It is reviewed against a speed
target, and a price that keeps chasing a target starts to behave like a cap.

## Pushed to the extreme, they predict different roads

These are thought experiments. Singapore runs both.

**Extreme COE is a buffet.** A huge fee at the door, every plate free after. Few
cars, but the 8am trip costs the owner nothing extra, so the few cars still
crowd the same roads at the same hour. If this were the whole story, speeds would
fall as more cars were packed onto each lane.

**Extreme ERP is surge pricing.** Anyone can own a car, but a jammed road at a
jammed hour costs more until it moves. The roads move. If this were the whole
story, speeds would stay inside the target band however crowded the roads got.

## My four bets

1. **The band holds** (35%). Every year, average peak speed sits inside LTA's
   target: 45 to 65 km/h on expressways, 20 to 30 km/h on arterial roads.
2. **Speed ignores crowding** (65%). Once road space is allowed for, peak speed
   barely moves as the number of cars per lane-kilometre changes.
3. **A dearer door does not cut driving** (20%). In the 7 years with the highest
   COE premium, the average car was driven no more than 3 per cent less than in
   the 7 cheapest.
4. **The price adds nothing beyond the count** (70%). Once the number of cars is
   known, the premium says nothing more about peak speed.

The percentages are how sure I was that each would hold. Together they put the
expected number holding at 1.9 of 4. The exact wording is in the
[sealed thesis](https://github.com/jacobbuildmodel/coe-analysis/blob/6d2a345d90015b8911e3be5ccfc76730a9d57206/erp/THESIS.md).

## The roads filled up and speeds held

![Peak-hour speed against LTA's target bands, 2004-2025, with cars per lane-km underneath](/figs/chart1_speed_vs_band.svg)

Between 2005 and 2017 the number of cars per lane-kilometre rose by 22 per cent
on expressways and 25 per cent on arterial roads. Under the buffet, that would
have shown up as slower traffic. It did not. On expressways, peak speed did not
move with crowding at all. On arterial roads it drifted up, not down, while
crowding rose. Bet 2 held on both road types.

That is the result ERP would produce if it works as designed. It is also the
result new rail lines, wider roads or a steady shift in when people travel would
produce. This is the thermostat problem: a thermostat that works leaves the room
at the same temperature whatever the weather, and a flat temperature record looks
exactly like a heater that did nothing on a mild day. A flat speed record cannot
tell a working controller from calm conditions.

## Where I was wrong

**Bet 1 broke on the fast side.** Expressways stayed inside 45 to 65 km/h. Arterial
roads did not: 30.4 km/h in 2016 and 31 km/h in 2023, both above the top of the
band. No year, on either road type, fell below its band. The buffet predicts
roads slower than the band; this miss is the opposite, arterial traffic moving
faster than LTA's target.

**Bet 3 missed, and I was right for the wrong reason.** I gave it only 20 per
cent, because the mileage data counts Grab and other hire cars with private cars,
and I expected their growth in the cheaper-COE years to make those years look
like heavier driving. The high-premium years did come out lower: 17,600 km per
car against 19,772, 11 per cent less, well past the 3 per cent line. But the hire
cars grew in the wrong years for my story. Hire and rental cars rose by 54,736
over the seven high-premium years and by only 5,591 over the seven cheap ones.
That pushed toward the bet holding, and it missed anyway.

![Kilometres per car in the seven dearest and seven cheapest COE years, 2005-2018](/figs/chart2_door_fee.svg)

What does line up with the split is time. All seven dear years fall between 2011
and 2017, and km per car fell in every year from 2007 to 2016, including years
when premiums were low. The data cannot separate "a pricier door cut driving"
from "driving per car was drifting down anyway".

**Bet 4 came out mixed.** On expressways, once the number of cars was known, the
COE premium added nothing. On arterial roads it added a little, and that little
was clear of zero. My own rule, written in advance, calls that neither a pass nor
a fail, so it does not count as holding.

**The tally.** 1 of 4 held against the 1.9 I expected. The Brier score, which
measures how far each confidence sat from what happened, was 0.194. A forecaster
who always says 50 per cent scores 0.25, and lower is better, but four
predictions is far too few to judge anyone's calibration.

## What would change my mind

- Peak speeds falling below the band for several years on either road type: the
  buffet showing through.
- Peak speed dropping at least one for one with cars per lane, consistently
  rather than in one odd year.
- A year-by-year record of how hard ERP was charging. Without it, half of the
  case for COE, that speeds tracked car numbers with ERP adding little, could not
  be tested at all.

## The verdict

The record cannot tell the two apart.

A pass for the ERP reading is weak evidence and a COE-like result is strong
evidence, because the design leans toward the ERP reading.

## What this does not explain

Speed is one part of what a car costs everyone else. Emissions, road accidents
and the land given over to roads and parking are the others, and none of them
was measured here. A verdict on speed is not a verdict on the whole cost of a
car. Nor does it say how hard ERP was pushing in any given year: no dated record
of ERP rate changes before mid-2018 could be found.

**The seal.** The four bets, their pass and fail lines and the confidences were
committed on 26 September 2026 in
[6d2a345](https://github.com/jacobbuildmodel/coe-analysis/blob/6d2a345d90015b8911e3be5ccfc76730a9d57206/erp/THESIS.md),
before any of the data files were opened, and the file has not changed since.
Anything learned afterwards is in
[THESIS_ADDENDUM.md](https://github.com/jacobbuildmodel/coe-analysis/blob/main/erp/THESIS_ADDENDUM.md),
and none of it changed a score.

**Years left out.** 2020 and 2021: ERP charging was suspended in 2020 in the same
weeks the circuit breaker emptied the roads, and 2021 still carried remote-work
restrictions. For bet 1 only, years whose speeds were already public before the
seal were also left out: 2011 for both road types, and 2019, 2022 and 2023 for
expressways.

**Conventions fixed after the seal.** A speed exactly on the edge of the band
counts as inside. Speeds are published in whole km/h from 2020, so two arterial
years recorded at exactly 30 could have been just above it; bet 1 fails either
way. Intervals are 90 per cent, with errors robust to uneven variance.

**Data caveats.** Road-length data exists only for 2005 to 2017, which limits bet 2
to those years. The mileage figures change from exact kilometres to round hundreds
in 2010, which may mark a change in how the survey was run; that could not be
confirmed from the source page, and it falls near the boundary between bet 3's two
groups. The speed figures are network averages, while LTA sets ERP rates gantry
by gantry and half-hour by half-hour, so a year can sit inside the band while
individual stretches do not. LTA's notes show peak speeds were averaged over the
financial year in the 2008 edition of its statistics and over the calendar year
by the 2015 edition; the date of the switch is not published. LTA's own lane-kilometre PDF differs from the
data.gov.sg file in one cell (arterial roads, 2008); using it barely moves bet 2.

**Reproduction.** One command rebuilds every number and chart from the raw files;
a second script recomputes every scored number by an independent route.
