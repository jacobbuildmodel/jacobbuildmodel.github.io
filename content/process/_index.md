Anyone can put numbers on a website. This page is here so you can decide whether to believe these
ones.

## Two rules cover almost everything

**Every number comes from a document you can check.** Company filings, government data, published
statistics. Where a figure comes from somewhere weaker, the page says so. Where a figure couldn't be
found at all, the page says that too, rather than reaching for an estimate that looks close enough.

**Every claim comes with what would prove it wrong.** Not a disclaimer, a specific observation: if
this number does that by this date, the argument was wrong. It's easy to write something that sounds
convincing and can never be checked. Naming what would break it makes that harder, which is the
point.

If a page here breaks either rule, it's a mistake rather than a style choice.

## What you'll find, and what you won't

Stock pages explain what a company does and how it makes money. Earnings pieces explain what a set of
results actually means. Economics pieces take a question people have opinions about and check it
against data.

**None of it tells you what to buy.** There's no overall score on any company, no ranking of one
against another, no price targets, and no buy or sell calls. That's a deliberate limit, not a gap
waiting to be filled.

The reason is partly legal, below, and partly that the useful part is the reasoning. A score is a
conclusion with the argument removed. If the reasoning is any good you can reach your own conclusion,
and if it isn't, a number wouldn't have saved it.

## Where the AI fits

Most pages here are produced with substantial help from AI systems I built and maintain. I write the
specifications, decide what counts as evidence, and do the editorial pass. The models do research and
drafting inside rules I set.

Saying so plainly matters more than it might seem. Nearly every rule on this site exists because an
early version produced something fluent and wrong, and I had to write a rule to stop it. That history
is the reason to trust the current version, not a reason to hide it.

Mistakes that reach publication are mine.

## Why this isn't financial advice

Under Singapore's Financial Advisers Act, a communication is generally treated as financial advice
when it expresses an opinion on whether to buy, sell or hold a specific investment, *and* a reasonable
reader could expect to rely on it. That turns on whether it's tailored to your circumstances, whether
it tells you to do something, and whether the writer holds themselves out as a professional adviser.
Naming a company isn't the deciding factor. Personalisation is.

So these aren't a workaround, they're what keeps this a research record rather than an advisory
service:

- **Nothing here is tailored.** Every reader sees the same page, whatever their goals, holdings,
  income or risk tolerance.
- **Nothing here tells you to act.** Mechanism, evidence, and what would prove it wrong. Never a buy,
  a sell, a price target or a position size.
- **I don't claim to be a professional.** No analyst title, no research house, no implied credential.
  One person's documented reasoning, published under his own name.
- **If you ask what to do with your own money, the answer is always the same:** I can't comment,
  because I'm not a licensed financial adviser. If you're weighing a real decision, a licensed adviser
  can account for your actual situation in a way a public page never can.

## The detail, if you want it

The two rules above apply everywhere. How they're applied differs by section, because reading a
company's accounts and running a regression are not the same discipline, and pretending otherwise
would be dishonest.

- **[What each score on a stock page means](/stocks/definitions/)** — every figure, its scale, where

None of it is required reading. It exists so that if you want to check the work, nothing is hidden.
