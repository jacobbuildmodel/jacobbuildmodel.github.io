> Palantir builds software that sits on top of all the other software an organisation already owns.
> It is one of the hardest companies in the market to explain, which is itself part of the story.

*Last filed quarter: three months to 30 June 2026 (Form 10-Q, filed 4 August 2026). Figures come from
that filing, the earnings release filed with it, or the 2025 annual report unless labelled otherwise.*

## What it actually sells

Palantir sells software that sits on top of all the *other* software an organisation already owns,
and answers questions that no single one of those systems can answer alone.

That sounds abstract, so here is the actual problem it solves.

An army has a database of personnel, a separate system tracking fuel, a maintenance log for vehicles,
a feed of satellite images, and radio reports coming in from units. Five systems, five formats, five
teams who built them at different times. Now the commander asks: *which of our units could actually
reach this location within twelve hours?* Answering it needs all five systems at once. Normally a
staff officer spends two days on the phone assembling the answer by hand.

Palantir's software builds a single shared dictionary of what all those systems *mean*: establishing
that this row in the fuel database refers to the same physical truck as that row in the maintenance
log, and that the truck belongs to the unit named in the third system. The company calls that
dictionary an **ontology**, a definition of the real-world things an organisation cares about — a
vehicle, a soldier, a shipment, a patient — and a mapping showing where each system holds a piece of
that thing. Once it exists, the twelve-hour question is asked once and answered in seconds. It also
means an AI model can be pointed at the organisation's real data and give an answer grounded in it,
rather than a plausible guess.

**What the customer physically gets is not a box.** Staff open a web application on an ordinary
computer. Behind that screen, Palantir employees — the company calls them *forward-deployed
engineers* — sit inside the customer's offices for months building that dictionary, because no two
organisations' systems are arranged the same way. This is why Palantir is not quite a normal software
company: a meaningful part of what it sells is people doing bespoke setup work.

The platform ships under a few names. **Gotham** for defence and intelligence work, **Foundry** for
companies, **AIP** for the AI layer added on top of both, and **Apollo**, the plumbing that delivers
software updates into secure environments not connected to the open internet.

## How a dollar arrives

A customer signs a contract, usually running several years, covering access to the platform plus the
engineering work to set it up. They pay on a schedule, frequently partly in advance. Palantir records
the revenue gradually, across the life of the contract, as it does the work.

**Does it repeat? Strongly, and that is the entire model.** The company reports **net dollar retention
of 157%**. That measures one thing: take the customers who were already there a year ago, ignore
every new customer won since, and compare what that same group paid this year against last. They paid
**57% more**. The strategy is to start small, one department and one problem, then grow inside the
account.

Two disclosed figures show money that has already changed hands or been committed.

- **Already collected but not yet earned:** deferred revenue plus customer deposits total about
  **$1.03 billion** sitting on the balance sheet. Customers paid; Palantir still owes them the work.
- **Committed but not yet delivered:** **$4.9 billion** of contracted revenue not yet recognised, of
  which 43% is expected within twelve months and 36% over the following two years.

**Now the part that most coverage skips.** The company also promotes two much larger numbers: *total
contract value* of $3.373 billion closed this quarter, and *remaining deal value* of $6.238 billion in
US commercial. Its own definition states that both figures "presume the exercise of all contract
options available to our customers and no termination of contracts," while acknowledging that "the
majority of our contracts are subject to termination provisions, including for convenience."

In plain English: the big headline numbers assume every customer takes every optional extension and
nobody walks away, while the company states elsewhere that most customers *can* walk away with notice.
The $4.9 billion figure is the stricter one, because it deliberately excludes anything cancellable.
When you see a Palantir backlog number quoted, it matters enormously which of the two it is.

## What the business is made of

**By customer type:**

| | Share of revenue | Contribution margin |
|---|---|---|
| Government | **51.2%** | 71% |
| Commercial (companies) | **48.8%** | 78% |

Contribution margin here is the company's own measure: what is left from each segment's revenue after
the direct costs of serving it, before company-wide overheads.

**Split four ways**, combining the segment note with the earnings release:

| | Share of revenue | Growth vs a year earlier |
|---|---|---|
| US government | **41.8%** | +90% |
| US commercial | **39.5%** | +149% |
| International government | ~9.4% | — |
| International commercial | ~9.4% | — |

**By geography:** United States **81%**, rest of world **19%**. A year ago that split was 73% / 27%.

The single most important thing in this table is that the growth is overwhelmingly American. US
revenue grew **115%** year on year; the rest of the world grew **34%**. Palantir is often described as
a global intelligence company, but almost all of its recent growth has come from inside one country.

## Who buys it, who competes

**Who buys it.** Palantir had **1,049 customers** in total, and **653** US commercial customers. Note
what that means: a company with billions in annual revenue serves roughly a thousand customers
worldwide. This is not a product sold to millions of people, it is sold to a small number of very
large institutions at very large prices. In this quarter alone it closed **220 deals worth at least $1
million each, 98 worth at least $5 million, and 73 worth at least $10 million**.

Palantir does not name customers in its financial filings. Government relationships become public
through the contracting process rather than through the company's own reporting, which is why the
defence work is widely known while the commercial customer list largely is not.

**Who competes, and here the answer is genuinely surprising.**

**Palantir's annual report does not name a single competitor.** What it says instead is this: *"We are
fundamentally competing with the internal software development efforts of our potential customers.
Organizations frequently attempt to build their own data platforms before turning to buy ours."* It
then describes competitors only by category: large enterprise software companies, government
contractors, and system integrators.

So the company's own stated main rival is its customers' in-house engineering departments, meaning the
option of not buying anything and building it yourself. Every other name commonly listed against
Palantir — Microsoft, Databricks and Snowflake on the corporate side, or Booz Allen Hamilton, Leidos
and Accenture Federal on the government side — reflects the market's view of who the alternatives are,
**not the company's own disclosure**. That distinction is worth holding onto, because the two lists
behave differently: a named rival can be beaten on price or features, while "the customer decides to
build it themselves" is beaten only by being faster than their own engineers.

## What would break it

**Customers can leave, and the company says so plainly.** Its own risk disclosures state that many
customer contracts "may be terminated by the customer at any time for convenience." This is a
structural feature of the business, not a warning about one bad quarter. Revenue that looks contracted
is, in large part, revenue that continues because the customer keeps choosing to continue.

**Just over half the revenue depends on government budgets.** Government is 51.2% of sales, and the US
government alone is 41.8%. That money moves with political appropriations and shifting priorities, not
with the economy. A budget freeze does not care how good the software is.

**The commercial surge is very new.** US commercial revenue grew 149% in a year. Growth that fast makes
the following year's comparison mechanically harder: to grow another 149%, it has to add more than
twice as much revenue in absolute terms as it just did.

**The model needs scarce people.** Engineers physically embed with customers for months, and the
company's own filings note that some staff must obtain security clearances before they can work on
certain contracts. Growth requires hiring and clearing more of these people, which is slower and more
expensive than shipping more copies of ordinary software.

**Building it yourself could get cheaper.** The company's stated main competitor is a customer's own
engineering team. As general-purpose AI coding tools improve, the in-house option gets cheaper and
faster, which pushes directly against Palantir's core pitch.

## How it's owned and controlled

There are three classes of share. Class A carries one vote. Class B carries ten. Then there is Class
F: just 1,005,000 shares, about **0.04% of all shares outstanding**, held in a voting trust
established by the three founders.

The filing states that Class F "generally gives the Founders the ability to control up to
**49.999999%** of the total voting power," provided they and their affiliates keep a minimum holding,
which was **100 million shares** as of 30 June 2026. That threshold is roughly **4% of the company**.

Ordinary shareholders can own the overwhelming majority of the economics and still not be able to
outvote the founders.

## What most people get wrong

**The 55% profit margin is mostly not from running the business.**

The quarter's revenue was $1.935 billion. Profit from operations, meaning actually selling and
delivering the software, was **$912 million, a 47% margin**. But the reported net profit was **$1.062
billion, a 55% margin**: *higher* than the operating profit, which is unusual and should always prompt
a look.

Two things create the gap. Interest and other income of about **$169 million**, earned on a $9.4
billion pile of cash and investments rather than on software. And tax: the company paid **$15.4 million
of tax on $1.081 billion of pre-tax profit, an effective rate of 1.4%**. The standard US federal
corporate rate is 21%. Whatever the reasons, a 55% net margin computed after a 1.4% tax charge is not a
margin that describes the operating business, and it is not one that survives a normal tax rate.

**The headline margin the company leads with is not the accounting one, and the difference is stock.**

Palantir promotes an adjusted operating margin of **62%**. The accounting figure is **47%**. The gap is
$282 million, and **$265 million of it, or 94%, is stock granted to employees**, which the adjusted
number removes. That stock is 13.7% of revenue, and it is a real cost: it is paid in new shares, so
every existing owner's slice gets slightly smaller.
