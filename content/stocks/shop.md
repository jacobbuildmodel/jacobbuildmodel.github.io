> On every $100 of goods sold through a Shopify shop, Shopify keeps about $3.10. Only 69 cents of
> that is the monthly software fee. The rest is a cut of the transaction, and that is the business.

*Last filed quarter: three months to 30 June 2026 (Form 10-Q, filed 5 August 2026). Every figure
below comes from that filing or from the annual report for 2025.*

## What it actually sells

Shopify sells the machinery behind an online shop, and then takes a cut of everything that shop
sells.

Start with the visible part. If you want to sell something online — hand-made candles, a clothing
label, spare car parts — you need a website that shows your products, a basket, a checkout that takes
card payments, a way to print postage labels, and a screen that tells you what sold. Building that
yourself means hiring developers. Shopify rents you the whole thing, ready-made, for a monthly fee.
You pick a design, upload photos and prices, and you have a shop.

But the monthly fee is the small half of this business, and that is the single most important thing
to understand about it.

The larger half is everything Shopify sells you *once your shop is running*. It processes the card
payments, which is the biggest one by far. It sells shipping labels at rates it negotiated in bulk.
It lends money to merchants against their future sales. It runs the card terminal if you also sell in
a physical shop. Each of these is priced as a slice of what passes through, so Shopify's income grows
when its merchants sell more, whether or not a single new merchant ever signs up.

So the honest one-line description is not "it sells shop software." It is: **Shopify rents you a
shop, and then charges you a small percentage of everything you sell through it.**

## How a dollar arrives

This is the section worth reading twice, because the answer is not the one the product suggests.

**Follow $100 of goods.** A customer buys $100 of candles from a shop that runs on Shopify. Of that
$100, Shopify keeps roughly **$3.10**. The merchant keeps the rest, and pays their own costs out of
it.

That $3.10 splits into two very unequal pieces:

- **About 69 cents is the subscription**, the merchant's monthly fee, spread across everything they
  sold that month.
- **About $2.41 is everything else**, mostly the fee for processing the card payment, plus shipping
  labels, lending and the rest.

Those figures are not estimates. In the quarter to 30 June 2026, **$115.6 billion** of goods was sold
through Shopify shops. Shopify's revenue on that was **$3.58 billion**, or 3.10% of it. Subscriptions
were $802 million of that revenue; everything else was $2.78 billion.

**So 78% of Shopify's revenue is a cut of sales, and only 22% is the software fee.** Most people have
it the other way round.

**Does it repeat?** Both halves do, but differently. The subscription repeats like rent: the company
reports **monthly recurring revenue of $221 million**, meaning that much arrives every month from
merchants simply staying subscribed. The larger half repeats only in the sense that shops keep
selling. It rises and falls with how much people are buying, week to week, with nothing contracted in
advance.

**The lever that matters most.** Shopify only earns the payment-processing fee when the merchant uses
*Shopify's own* payment system rather than someone else's. In the latest quarter, **68% of everything
sold ran through Shopify Payments**, up from 64% a year earlier. That four-point move is worth a great
deal: payments revenue alone rose by $624 million year over year. Watching that percentage is the
closest thing to watching the engine directly.

## What the business is made of

Shopify reports two segments, and the company's own names for them are unhelpful, so here is what
each actually contains.

| | Share of revenue | What it is | Growth | Gross margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Merchant solutions** | **78%** | The cut of sales: payment processing, shipping labels, lending, card terminals | +37% | **38%** |
| **Subscription solutions** | **22%** | The monthly fee for the shop software itself | +22% | **80%** |

Gross margin here means what is left from each dollar of that revenue after the direct cost of
providing it, before the company's own overheads.

Read the last two columns together, because they pull in opposite directions. **The part growing
fastest is also the part that keeps least.** Merchant solutions keeps 38 cents of each dollar;
subscriptions keep 80 cents. As the payments business grows faster than the software business, the
average margin across the whole company drifts down, which is exactly what happened this quarter:
gross margin was 47.7%, against 48.6% a year earlier.

**Where the merchants are**, by location, from the annual report: 44% United States, 31% Europe,
Middle East and Africa, 16% Asia Pacific, 5% Canada, 5% Latin America.

## Who buys it, who competes

**Who buys it.** Millions of merchants across more than 175 countries, from one person selling
candles to large brands running their own direct-to-customer operations. That breadth is the defining
feature: no individual merchant matters much, so Shopify's fortunes track the health of small and
medium online retail as a whole rather than any single relationship.

Shopify does not name individual merchants in its financial filings.

**Who competes, and the answer is revealing.**

**Shopify's annual report does not name a single competitor.** Instead it lists the *kinds* of company
a merchant might buy from instead. There are eleven of them: ecommerce software vendors, content
management systems, payment processors, point-of-sale providers, domain registrars, shipping-label
providers, fulfilment services, alternative lenders, financial services, cross-border services
providers, and marketplaces.

That list is worth sitting with, because it is the clearest statement of what the business actually
is. **Shopify has eleven categories of competitor because it sells eleven different things.** A
merchant can leave any one piece — take payments elsewhere, buy postage elsewhere, borrow elsewhere —
without leaving the shop software. Each departure removes a slice of that $3.10.

Any specific company you see listed against Shopify reflects the market's view of the alternatives,
not the company's own disclosure.

## What would break it

**Consumer spending falls.** Because most revenue is a percentage of sales rather than a fixed fee, a
downturn in online shopping hits Shopify immediately and directly. It does not need to lose a single
merchant to lose revenue.

**Merchants take the payments elsewhere.** The payment-processing share is the most valuable thing
Shopify has and the least contractually protected. That 68% figure could fall as easily as it rose,
and the revenue attached to it is roughly three times the entire subscription business.

**A large platform bundles it in.** The annual report explicitly worries about this: a competitor with
a strong position in a neighbouring market integrating comparable features into a product it already
controls, or changing the terms on which Shopify's merchants can reach customers.

**Lending losses.** Shopify advances money to merchants against future sales. The cost of that going
wrong is a real line in the accounts: transaction and loan losses were **$141 million** in the
quarter, up from $80 million a year earlier, growing faster than revenue. In a recession, small
retailers are precisely the borrowers who struggle.

**The margin mix keeps drifting.** Nothing dramatic has to happen for the average margin to keep
falling. It falls simply because the lower-margin half keeps growing faster, which is the current,
ordinary state of the business.

## What most people get wrong

**It is mostly a payments company wearing a software company's clothes.**

The product you can see is the shop builder. The money is somewhere else. As the dollar mechanics
above show, 78% of revenue is a cut of transactions, and the single largest component of that is card
processing. If you described Shopify to someone as a payments business that gives away shop software
to win the processing, you would be closer to the accounts than the usual description is.

**The profit figure in the headlines is not from selling anything.**

In the quarter to June 2026, Shopify made **$488 million** operating profit, which is the actual
business of running shops and processing payments. But reported net profit was **$1.50 billion**,
three times higher. The difference is almost entirely **$1,249 million of unrealised gains on shares
Shopify owns in other companies**. Unrealised means the shares went up on paper; nothing was sold and
no cash arrived.

This swings violently in both directions. In the first half of 2025 the same line was a *loss* of $340
million. Anyone tracking Shopify's profitability by its headline net income is mostly tracking someone
else's share price.

**"More merchants" is not the growth story.**

The intuitive model, sign up more shops and earn more subscriptions, describes the smaller and
slower-growing half. What actually moves the numbers is existing merchants selling more, and a rising
share of those sales running through Shopify's own payment system. Goods sold through the platform
grew 33% over the half-year; the subscription line grew 22%. **The business grows mainly because its
customers grow, not because it adds customers.**
