> Nvidia makes a specialised kind of computer chip that turns out to be exactly what artificial
> intelligence needs. Almost every AI system you've heard of runs on its hardware.

## What it actually sells

A chip roughly the size of a postage stamp, mounted on a circuit board about the size of a
paperback book, which slots into a server in a data centre.

The chip does one thing extremely well: enormous numbers of simple calculations, all at the same
time. An ordinary computer processor does a few complicated things very fast, one after another.
Nvidia's chips do millions of simple things simultaneously. That turns out to be exactly the shape
of the maths that AI models need, which is why they ended up owning this market largely by accident
after starting out making chips for video games.

It also sells the software that makes those chips usable. That part matters more than it sounds.

## How a dollar arrives

A company building AI systems, or renting out computing power to others, buys a rack of servers
containing Nvidia chips. They pay once, up front, and the chips physically arrive.

This is not a subscription. There is no recurring payment. Every quarter, Nvidia has to sell new
chips to new orders to keep revenue up, which is why any hint that customers might slow their
spending moves the shares so hard.

The customers are not consumers. A handful of very large technology companies account for a
disproportionate share of what Nvidia sells.

## What the business is made of

Data centre chips are the overwhelming majority of revenue. Gaming, the original business, is now
a modest slice. Smaller amounts come from chips for cars and for professional design work.

The concentration is the point: this is essentially one business with some historical attachments.

## Who buys it, who competes

Buyers are the large cloud providers and AI companies building out data centres.

Competitors fall into two groups. There are other chipmakers selling rival products. More
interestingly, several of Nvidia's largest customers are designing their own chips in-house
specifically to reduce how much they need to buy from Nvidia.

## What would break it

The customers are the competition. The same handful of companies that make up much of Nvidia's
revenue are also the ones with the money and the motive to build alternatives.

The other risk is simpler: this is a business that sells equipment for a construction boom. If
companies decide they've built enough AI capacity for now, orders don't slow gently. They stop.

## What most people get wrong

That the moat is the chip. It mostly isn't.

Nvidia's software has been the standard way to program these chips for well over a decade, which
means an enormous amount of the world's AI code is written against it. A rival chip that matches
on raw speed still asks customers to rewrite work they've already done. That switching cost, not
raw performance, is the harder thing to dislodge.

The second thing people miss: Nvidia's biggest risk and its biggest customers are the same list of
companies.
