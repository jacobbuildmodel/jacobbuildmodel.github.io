> NVIDIA makes a computer chip that turned out to be exactly what artificial intelligence needed.
> It sells them to a very small number of very large buyers, and those buyers are increasingly
> building their own alternatives.

*Last filed quarter: three months to 26 April 2026 (Form 10-Q, filed 20 May 2026). Figures come from
that filing or the annual report to 25 January 2026 unless labelled otherwise.*

## What it actually sells

NVIDIA sells a computer chip, and increasingly, an entire cabinet of computers built around those
chips.

The chip is a **GPU**, a graphics processing unit. It began life as the part of a computer that
draws images for video games. Drawing an image means doing the same simple piece of arithmetic on
millions of pixels at the same moment, so the chip was built to do thousands of small calculations
simultaneously rather than a few large ones very quickly. It turned out that teaching an AI model
works the same way: millions of tiny, repetitive sums. A company that made gaming parts found itself
holding the exact tool the AI industry needed.

What a buyer physically receives today is usually not one chip. NVIDIA's main product is a **rack**:
a metal cabinet roughly the size of a large refrigerator, filled with boards carrying its chips, plus
its own cables and switches so the chips can talk to one another fast enough to work as a single
machine. A data-centre operator wheels the cabinet into a room, connects power and network, and then
rents out time on it.

There is also software, called **CUDA**. NVIDIA gives it away free. It is the toolkit programmers use
to write instructions these chips understand. It runs on NVIDIA hardware and nothing else, so every
hour a programmer spends learning it is an hour invested in NVIDIA specifically.

## How a dollar arrives

**Someone buys a box, once.** There is no subscription and no monthly fee.

A company that builds or runs data centres places an order, takes delivery of hardware, and pays for
it. NVIDIA counts the sale when the hardware ships. Next quarter, that same customer has to place
another order, or the revenue from them is zero.

Here is the number that makes this concrete. Companies disclose how much revenue is already locked
into signed contracts but not yet delivered. For NVIDIA that figure is **$2.6 billion**, against
**$81.6 billion** of sales in the most recent quarter alone. So contracted future revenue amounts to
about **3% of a single quarter's sales**. Roughly 97% of next quarter's revenue does not yet exist in
any signed agreement. It has to be sold again, from scratch, every three months.

One wrinkle worth knowing. NVIDIA splits its buyers into two groups. **Direct customers** are the
ones that actually pay NVIDIA, and these are frequently middlemen: board manufacturers,
distributors, contract assemblers, and firms that build finished systems. **Indirect customers** are
the organisations that end up using the machine, buying through those middlemen. When you read that a
famous technology company is a huge NVIDIA customer, that relationship may well sit in the second
group, and NVIDIA does not confirm names in either.

## What the business is made of

NVIDIA publishes two different splits of the same quarter. Both are shown, because they answer
different questions and they do not reconcile.

**By product platform, meaning what the machine is for:**

| | Share of revenue |
|---|---|
| Data Center | **92.2%** |
| — Hyperscale (the largest cloud operators) | 46.4% of total |
| — AI Clouds, Industrial & Enterprise | 45.8% of total |
| Edge Computing (PCs, consoles, workstations, robots, cars) | **7.8%** |

**By reporting segment, meaning how the company manages itself:**

| | Share of revenue |
|---|---|
| Compute & Networking | **91.3%** |
| Graphics (gaming and workstation cards) | **8.7%** |

NVIDIA changed how it presents the platform split this quarter and restated prior periods to match.
Gaming is no longer a separately disclosed platform line.

**By where the customer is headquartered:** United States 78.1%, Taiwan 14.7%, China including Hong
Kong 5.6%, everywhere else 1.6%.

Sales to customers headquartered outside the United States were **22%** of revenue this quarter, down
from **42%** a year earlier. The business has become dramatically more American in twelve months.

## Who buys it, who competes

**Who buys it: NVIDIA will not say.**

The filing calls them Customer A, Customer B and Customer C. Those three accounted for **21%, 17% and
16% of total revenue**, or **54% between them**. A year earlier, two customers were 16% and 14%, so
30% combined. Three buyers whose names are not public now provide more than half the money.

**Who competes: here NVIDIA is specific.** Its annual report lists competitors by category.

- **Chips and accelerated computing:** AMD, Huawei, Intel
- **Large cloud companies designing their own chips in-house:** Alibaba, Alphabet, Amazon, Baidu,
  Huawei, Microsoft
- **Arm-based processors:** Amazon, Huawei, Microsoft
- **System-on-chip products:** Ambarella, AMD, Broadcom, Intel, Qualcomm, Renesas, Samsung, and
  Tesla for chips it designs for its own vehicles

**Read those two lists together.** Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft appear on NVIDIA's own competitor
list. They are also, on any reasonable reading, among its largest buyers. NVIDIA's biggest customers
and its most capable rivals are, to a significant degree, the same handful of companies. That is
unusual, and it means the customer risk and the competition risk are not two separate problems. They
are one problem.

## What would break it

**A big buyer builds its own.** Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft are already designing their own AI
chips, which is precisely why they appear on the competitor list. Because three customers are 54% of
revenue, a single decision inside a single company could remove a fifth of the business.

**The construction slows down.** Data-centre buying is capital spending: lumpy, discretionary, and
easy to postpone. With only 3% of a quarter's revenue contracted in advance, a pause in orders
reaches the income statement almost immediately. There is no subscription base to cushion it.

**The margin closes.** NVIDIA keeps about **74.9 cents of every dollar** of sales after the direct
cost of making the product. That gap survives only while no rival product is good enough to force a
price cut.

**Manufacturing.** NVIDIA designs chips, it does not own the factories that make them. Production is
concentrated overwhelmingly in Taiwan.

**It has happened before.** In the year to January 2023, revenue was essentially flat, up 0.2%, while
profit fell by about 55%. Sales held up and earnings still halved.

## What most people get wrong

**The profit is no longer purely from selling chips.** In the latest quarter, profit from the actual
business of designing and selling hardware, meaning operating profit, was **$53.5 billion**. But the
headline net profit was **$58.3 billion**, which is *higher*. The difference is $15.9 billion of
income from outside normal operations, and the filing states that **$13.4 billion of it was
unrealised gains on shares NVIDIA owns in other companies**. Unrealised means on paper: the shares
went up, nothing was sold. Headline profit grew **211%** against a year earlier, while profit from
the operating business grew **147%**. NVIDIA has become, in part, an investor in the industry it
supplies, and those investments now visibly move its reported earnings.

**"NVIDIA sells to everyone" is wrong, and getting more wrong.** As covered in the dollar mechanics
above, concentration is rising rather than falling: two customers at 30% combined a year ago, three
at 54% today. On money billed but not yet collected the picture is tighter still, with three
customers accounting for **30%, 18% and 16% of receivables**, or **64%**.

## On competitive protection

NVIDIA's gross margin was **74.9%** while revenue grew **85%** year on year, and the company has
guided it to stay near 75%. A business with no protection has to cut prices to grow quickly. NVIDIA
nearly doubled its sales in a year and kept the same margin, which means buyers paid full price
because there was no equal alternative.

The CUDA software toolkit reinforces this: it runs only on NVIDIA hardware, so programmer skill
accumulates in a form that does not transfer to a rival's chips.

The qualifier matters. The three largest buyers are also on the company's own competitor list and are
actively building alternatives. That describes the position today, not ten years out.
