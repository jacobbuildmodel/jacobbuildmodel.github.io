> **Coverage:** 10–14 August 2026 (trading days), tape swept through 17 August.

Gold closed the week near the top of its range. US investors spent the week taking money *out* of it —
$365.5m redeemed from the largest gold ETF in a single day, and $6.7bn year-to-date. Both things are
true, which means somebody other than the American ETF buyer is setting the gold price right now. That
gap is the most interesting thing in the week, and it isn't visible on a chart.

Below: what the market was paying for, where the dollars actually went, what's dated and coming, three
things I'm researching, and the list of figures I couldn't get.

## The regime

The factor being paid this week was **value and small-cap, and it was paid by the removal of a rate
hike.**

The July FOMC held 9–3, with three dissents *for a quarter-point hike* — the first three-way
same-direction dissent since 2016. That dissent was the live risk coming into the week, and the week
took it off the table: July CPI printed +0.1% month-on-month and +3.4% year-on-year, core +0.2% and
+2.6%, and PPI came in softer than expected. September hike probability fell from roughly 55% to about
30% {{< tag "consensus" >}}.

Small caps hold the most floating-rate debt and the longest duration of earnings, so they were the
direct beneficiary. The Russell 2000 set fresh intraday and closing records on 14 August, on a session
where the S&P 500 *fell* 0.17% and the Nasdaq fell 0.28%. That divergence is the regime in one line.

What cuts the other way is the consumer. July retail sales fell 0.6% to $763.6bn against a +0.1%
consensus {{< tag "hard-co" >}} — the largest drop in over a year — and preliminary August consumer
sentiment came in at 51 against a forecast of 55. That follows a payrolls print of **−23,000** against
a forecast near +83,000, with the prior two months revised down by a combined 103,000.

So: the hike is off, and the growth question is now open. Long-dated yields stayed elevated on
fiscal-borrowing supply, which is why this reads as a rotation rather than a duration rally.

## Where the money actually went

Roughly $50bn flowed into US-listed ETFs in the week ended 13 August, of which equity took ~$31bn and
domestic equity $23bn {{< tag "hard-flow" >}}. The largest creations were SPY, QQQ, TLT, VOO, SGOV and
EFV.

The change worth carrying is a deceleration one tier down: total ETF net issuance for the prior full
week was $43.70bn, with equity issuance falling from $40.53bn to $29.80bn week-on-week
{{< tag "hard-flow" >}}. Money is still arriving. It is arriving more slowly.

Two places where price and flow disagreed. I lead with flow in both.

**Gold.** Spot finished 14 August up 0.38%, near the top of its range. The largest gold ETF was
redeemed −$365.5m on 13 August, the second-largest −$74.9m, and the commodity ETF class was net
−$275.1m on the day. Year-to-date, that first fund carries **$6.7bn of net outflows** despite the
metal's run {{< tag "hard-flow" >}}. This isn't a one-week wobble — the same fund appeared among the
week's top *inflows*, so the two data points are measuring different windows and both are real. The
read: the gold price is not being funded by US ETF demand, which makes it a weaker signal for
gold-linked equities than the spot price alone suggests.

**Semiconductors.** On 13 August, unlevered semi exposure was created +$410.3m while *leveraged* semi
exposure was redeemed — −$57.0m and −$61.4m across two vehicles. Market-wide, the leveraged ETF class
saw −$126.0m and the inverse class **+$199.7m, or +1.57% of its assets** — the largest
assets-relative move of any class that day. Broadcom then fell 5.3–5.9% on 14 August. Price says semis
were sold. Flow says leverage was retired while unlevered exposure was accumulated. Those are
different events, and only the first shows up on a chart.

**One flow that means nothing.** Reddit entered the S&P 500 before the open on 18 August and ran
10–15% on the announcement. The index buying that follows is mechanical creation against a fixed
weight — not informational demand. Worth naming so it doesn't get scored as conviction.

**Q2 13F filings**, published 14 August, gave the week's only position-level disclosure. Across 6,371
filers: 44% trimmed Magnificent Seven holdings against 42% adding; semis were still net-bought 48% to
34.5%; a software group was net-sold 28.2% to 26.3%; and **energy was net-sold 40.3% to 28.0% despite
the Q2 crude spike** {{< tag "hard-flow" >}}. That last one is the residual. Institutions were net
sellers of energy into a quarter when crude rose and Gulf shipping broke. That's a positioning gap,
not a fundamental view.

## Research watchlist

Three items. Two outside technology, one under $20bn. None of this is a recommendation — each is a
question I'm trying to answer, with the observation that would kill it.

### 1. Tanker earnings are splitting in two: crude holds, products roll over {{< tag "verified" >}}

**Mechanism.** International Seaways disclosed forward Q3 bookings *on its earnings call* — not as a
filing line item. Q2 realised rates: VLCC $118,883/day, Suezmax $100,543/day, LR1 $79,180/day, MR
$60,342/day. Q3 already booked: 49% of VLCC days at $118,300/day and 55% of Suezmax days at
$91,800/day — flat to Q2 — but only $37,200/day on LR1s and $34,700/day on MRs. That's roughly a 50%
step down in the product fleet while the crude fleet holds
{{< tag "company-prelim" >}}.

Crude freight is being held up by the Hormuz risk premium and re-routing distance. Refined-product
freight is not. Any model treating "tankers" as one exposure is wrong by half.

**Tickers.** INSW, FRO, STNG — STNG is the pure product read, and where the step-down should surface
first.

**The question I'm researching.** Whether the product-tanker step-down is a booking-timing artefact or
a genuine rate collapse. The crude/product spread widening this fast is unusual enough that I want a
second operator confirming it.

**Falsifier.** Any product-tanker operator guiding Q3 MR or LR1 rates above $50k/day, or spot MR
re-crossing $50k/day. Window: Q3 prints, late October.

### 2. The largest industrial buyer of silver is engineering it out — and confirmed it while silver was falling {{< tag "inferred" >}}

**Mechanism.** Pandora, the world's largest jewellery brand by volume, said on 13 August that its
shift to platinum-plating is proceeding *despite falling silver prices*, targeting silver at roughly
20% of merchandise by 2028 {{< tag "company-prelim" >}}. Consumer research it cited found 78%
recognise platinum as a precious metal versus 69% for sterling silver.

This matters because it's **price-insensitive demand destruction** — a design decision taken on
volatility rather than level, which means it doesn't reverse when the price falls. The consensus
silver bull case rests on industrial demand being price-inelastic. This is the first large, dated,
public counter-example.

**Tickers.** Pandora is Copenhagen-listed and **not directly investable** for a US book. Closest
proxies: SLV and SIL on the short side of the thesis, PPLT on the long side, PAAS and HL as the
operating-leverage expressions.

**The question I'm researching.** Whether one brand's substitution is large enough to move aggregate
jewellery offtake, or whether I'm extrapolating a single company's design decision into a sector
claim. The mechanism is solid; the magnitude is mine and unverified.

**Falsifier.** Pandora reversing or diluting the 2028 target at any quarterly update, or the next
Silver Institute demand survey showing jewellery offtake flat-to-up.

*A note on rigour:* two sources this week put spot silver at $64.81/oz and ~$75/oz. I couldn't
reconcile them, so **no silver price level carries any weight in this piece.** The mechanism doesn't
depend on the level.

### 3. AI accelerator capex may be migrating into leveraged leasing {{< tag "speculative" >}}

**Mechanism.** A sell-side note on 14 August estimated that a chip-financing vehicle attached to
Broadcom's custom AI accelerator business could carry substantial senior debt by 2029 at scale — and
critically, that debt would not sit on Broadcom's balance sheet. The vehicle raises capital and leases
accelerators to customers {{< tag "consensus" >}}.

If a material share of AI compute is bought with third-party lease financing rather than hyperscaler
operating cash flow, then AI capex durability becomes a function of credit spreads and issuance
capacity. The leading indicator stops being hyperscaler guidance and starts being investment-grade and
high-yield issuance windows. That's a re-rating of *what to watch*, and it isn't obtainable from a
10-Q, because the leverage isn't in one.

**Tickers.** Not cleanly expressible. Closest are private-credit and asset-management lenders — ARES,
APO, BX — with LQD and HYG spread behaviour as the observable. Broadcom is named here as the cause,
not as an idea.

**The question I'm researching.** Whether the structure exists at the scale described. This is a
single sell-side estimate with no filed row supporting or contradicting it, which is why it's tagged
speculative. **The structure is the claim; the number is not**, and I've deliberately left the
headline figure out of this post until I can source it to something primary.

**Falsifier.** The structure being brought on balance sheet, materially downsized, or explicitly
disowned; or 2027 issuance guidance coming in far below the estimate. Window: Nvidia's 26 August call
and Broadcom's September quarter — both will be asked.

## Sector read

I read the same twelve bellwethers every week — XOM, JPM, UNH, CAT, COST, NEE, FCX, PLD, HD, UNP, LMT
and EEM — so that changes are visible over time rather than reconstructed. Four moved the directional
read this week; the rest produced nothing worth reporting.

**Shipping and ports.** DP World's H1: Q2 volumes at Jebel Ali fell 90.1% year-on-year to 374,000 teu,
after a 30.5% Q1 drop. Group throughput was −22.0% like-for-like in H1 — but **+3.6% excluding Jebel
Ali.** H1 profit −39.1% to $585m on revenue +13.1% {{< tag "hard-co" >}}. The read: Gulf trade isn't
slowed, it's severed at one node, and the group is building around it with new terminals to bypass
Hormuz. This *complicates* the tanker item above — the freight windfall and the port collapse are the
same event, and a Hormuz reopening reverses both at once.

**Consumer.** Retail sales −0.6%, sentiment 51 versus 55. This complicates the regime read directly:
small-cap value leadership and a rolling consumer aren't durably compatible. This is the main way the
week's call breaks.

**Software.** Workday is in takeover talks at ~$51bn; the stock rose ~18% then gave back 4.5%.
Supports the value read — large-cap software being valued as an acquisition asset rather than a growth
asset is a value signal, not a growth one.

**EM and LatAm.** Nu Holdings posted its first billion-dollar quarterly net income and rose ~11–12%,
while the main Brazil ETF was redeemed −$98.3m {{< tag "hard-flow" >}}. Fundamentals up, dollars out.
Flagged because flow and fundamentals disagree, and flow is the side I lead with.
