# QUESTION CRAFT: ask it the way an economics exam does (adopted 4 Nov 2026, Jacob)

Adopted by Jacob on 4 November 2026. Every question and header on this site, and in every brief and research
note behind it, follows the pattern of A-level economics exam questions: clear, specific, solution-oriented and
actionable, backed by named economic concepts, and using AI and advanced analysis where they help. Teaser headers
and rhetorical questions hide the subject; they are not used. office/QUESTION_RULES.md rules 1-9 still govern the
sealed question in THESIS.md.

## 1. The pattern (how the exam questions are set)
- **Header = the topic, plainly.** A noun phrase naming the market or policy: "Land transport in Singapore", "The
  central bus contracting model", "Skills training". Never a question, a pun or a cliffhanger.
- **Stem = 2-4 factual sentences.** A named actor, what it does, one fact with a number or date ("fares can be
  double", "more than S$2 billion a year"). The stem carries the lived moment; no question in it.
- **Part (a) Explain = the mechanism.** "Explain why [actor] is able to [do X] and how [X] affects [outcome]",
  "Using a diagram, explain why [market outcome] may be [allocatively inefficient]", "Using a relevant elasticity
  concept, explain the extent to which [policy] would [change behaviour]". It names ONE economic concept.
- **Part (b) Discuss = the decision.** "Discuss whether [option A] or [option B] is more effective to [objective]",
  "Discuss whether [policy] is the most appropriate policy to achieve [economic efficiency] in [market]", "Discuss
  the likely impact of [quoted policy change] on [efficiency] in [industry]". Two concrete options, one criterion.
- **Every question names four things:** the actor or market, the policy or change, the economic concept, and the
  criterion (efficiency, equity, profits, total revenue, market failure, real purchasing power, employment).
- **One directive verb first** (Explain / Using ... explain / Discuss whether / Discuss the likely impact / Assess
  the extent to which). The exact policy phrase from the stem is quoted when it matters.

## 2. How the site uses it
- **Research question (brief, CP0 note, open-question page):** header, stem, (a), (b), then two lines the exam does
  not need but the site does: **Method** (the identification strategy and where AI or advanced analysis helps: e.g.
  difference-in-differences, event study, regression discontinuity, synthetic control, Bayesian structural time
  series, causal forest for heterogeneous effects, LLM-assisted coding of Hansard or occupations, hand-checked) and
  **Decision** (who decides, when, and what each result would change). Sealed bets are predictions inside (a) and (b).
- **Article title:** the (b) question compressed, or its answer stated with the concept, under 60 characters
  ("COE or road pricing: which corrects congestion better?"). The context stem is the first paragraph.
- **Section headings:** plain topic phrases that say what the section holds ("The mechanism: tax incidence on power
  bills", "The policy choice: rebates or regulation"). No teasers, no rhetorical questions.
- **Idea lists and cards:** each item is header + stem + (a) + (b) + Method + Decision. A title alone must let Jacob
  know the market, the policy and the question.
- **Questions to Jacob (AskUserQuestion, wrap-up box):** one decision; name the thing in plain words; give the
  options and what each leads to; no internal codes (no "r3", "PR A", "lens", "card id") unless the link is given.
  Model: "Should the SGD note go to coe-analysis now (published 14 Nov) or wait for another review?"

## 3. Solution-oriented, not just descriptive
- A question that only asks "did X happen?" is part (a) at most. Every piece needs a part (b): which policy or design
  works better, by what criterion, and what the evidence would change for the decision-maker.
- Name the trade-off (efficiency vs equity, short run vs long run, cost to government vs deadweight loss).
- The site never advises a reader: (b) evaluates policies; no "you should" in the article (Vale Directives).

## 4. Usable terms (use the exact term, then a plain gloss once)
Market failure: negative/positive externality, public good, merit good, information failure (asymmetric
information, adverse selection, moral hazard), principal-agent problem. Efficiency: allocative, productive,
dynamic, deadweight loss, X-inefficiency. Elasticity: price elasticity of demand and supply, cross-price, income.
Incidence and pass-through (taxes, subsidies, vouchers, exchange rates). Market structure: barriers to entry,
contestability, economies of scale, price discrimination (first, second, third degree), monopsony. Labour: marginal
revenue product, substitutes vs complements, lump-of-labour fallacy, reservation wage, search frictions,
structural vs frictional unemployment, labour force participation, human capital. Policy: Pigouvian tax or subsidy,
quota, deposit-refund system, tradable permits, regulation, government failure, regulatory capture, crowding out,
opportunity cost. Macro and money: real vs nominal, NEER/REER, purchasing power, demand-pull vs cost-push inflation,
terms of trade. Pick the one the question turns on; do not stack them.

## 5. Checks before it goes out
1. Read the header alone: does it name the topic? 2. Read the question alone: does it name actor, policy, concept,
criterion? 3. Is there a part (b) with two concrete options? 4. Is the Method line real (data exists, labels
checked, no outcome values opened before a seal: PURPOSE_PRINCIPLES G0)? 5. Is every number in the stem sourced and
dated? 6. Public strings still get the wording review and the truth pass; the purpose gate still comes
first (words never rescue a weak question). 7. Sealed THESIS wording keeps QUESTION_RULES rules 1-9 and is never
edited after the seal.
