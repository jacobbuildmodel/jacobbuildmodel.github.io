# STORY_CRAFT -- how a piece reads (companion to QUESTION_RULES and STANDARDS)

From a case study of three short-form research posts (28 Sep 2026) and openings
borrowed from literature (29 Sep 2026). Rigour rules (pre-registration,
failures first, no advice) always win over any craft rule below.

## Adopt

S1. Specimen before statistics: one concrete, real, dated case in two or three
    short sentences. A thought experiment is allowed if labelled ("Picture...");
    never an invented person quoted as real.
S2. Lead with the puzzle the reader feels, in short sentences, before the question.
S3. Show, then name: the idea through the example first, then named once in
    plain words. One named idea per piece; it goes on the Ideas page.
S4. Headings carry the story (articles): spine label plus the finding.
S5. Answer the obvious objection out loud, in the reader's words.
S6. Show where the finding flips, with the numbers.
S7. One compression line, literally true; no word stronger than the evidence.
S8. Captions tell the reader how to read each chart.
S9. Serialise: every article points to the next open question; every open page
    links back to the last answer.
S10. Rhythm, sparingly: short sentences after long ones.

## Openings borrowed from literature

L1. The belief everyone holds, stated with a wink (Austen, 1813: "It is a truth
    universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good
    fortune, must be in want of a wife."). State the reader's belief as settled,
    then turn it.
L2. The paradox that demands a resolution (Dickens, 1859: "It was the best of
    times, it was the worst of times.").
L3. The whole argument in one line (Tolstoy, 1878, Garnett tr.: "Happy families
    are all alike; every unhappy family is unhappy in its own way.").
L4. A voice, close up (Melville, 1851: "Call me Ishmael.").
L5. The ordinary scene with one detail wrong.
L6. The impossible, said flatly, without "shockingly" or "surprisingly".
L7. The ending told first: in answer articles, the verdict in the first line.
Guardrails: every sentence literally true or labelled as a thought experiment;
no invented quotes from real people; the device serves the evidence.

## Reject

R1. No advice. "You" only inside a labelled thought experiment or a scene the
    reader recognises, never to tell them what to do.
R2. No post-hoc flips presented as findings.
R3. No stacked one-sided evidence.
R4. No overclaims ("confirms", "proves", causal verbs the design cannot license).
R5. No jargon without a same-sentence translation.
R6. No engagement bait at the end.
R7. Headline numbers carry their cost (n, interval, drawdown).
