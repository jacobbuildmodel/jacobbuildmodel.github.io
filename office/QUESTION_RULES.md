# QUESTION RULES -- how every question, headline and tagline on the site is written

Adopted 24 September 2026, approved by Jacob. Revised the same day after Jacob
rejected the checker's piece-2 options: rules 1-9 were being applied to a weak
topic. Rule 0 now comes first. Revised 29 September 2026 after Jacob rejected the
checker's Singapore-dollar titles as technical and feelingless: the HOOK rules
(H1-H7) now govern every title and first paragraph; the exam rules govern the
sealed question in THESIS.md. Applies to: research questions in briefs and
THESIS.md, the "question" line above each headline, headlines, section intros,
the home header, roadmap items. Every brief and every checkpoint checks against
this list.

## Two layers, two jobs

| Layer | Job | Governed by |
|---|---|---|
| Title and first paragraph (what the reader sees) | Make a person feel "wait, really?" and know within one paragraph what the piece is about | H1-H7 |
| Sealed question in THESIS.md, tests, verdict | Be precise, contestable, testable, with a yardstick | Rule 0, rules 1-9 |

Rigour lives in the sealed layer. The title never carries jargon or a
yardstick the reader would not say out loud.

## Why the checker's titles failed (29 September 2026)

"Does a stronger Singapore dollar actually keep prices down?" and "When import
prices jump, how much does the Singapore dollar actually block?" were rejected:
- Written from the method outward: they named what the test measures, not a
  moment anyone has lived.
- No person in them. Nobody thinks about "prices" in general; they think about
  their Japan trip, their JB grocery run, their savings.
- No belief on trial. The reader's own idea ("our dollar is strong because our
  economy is strong") was not named, so there was nothing to be surprised by.
- Analyst words. "Block", "import prices", "yardstick" carry no feeling.
- Too little of the story: from the title alone a reader could not guess 60 per
  cent of the piece.

## HOOK rules (titles and first paragraphs) -- H1-H7

- H1. Start from a moment the reader has lived. A Japan trip, a JB grocery run,
  a payslip, a hawker plate, a car ad, an ERP gantry. Not the policy.
- H2. Put their belief on trial. Name what most people assume and ask if it is
  true. Us-or-them, this-or-that, in their words.
- H3. The 60/90 rule. The title tells a reader 60 per cent of what the piece is
  about. By the end of the first paragraph they know 90 per cent, and can decide
  whether they are in.
- H4. Feeling words over mechanism words. No term the reader would not say to a friend.
- H5. Short form: the answer early. An answer article gives the verdict in the
  first screen; an open question page gives the bet in the first screen.
- H6. Personal, not political. About what Singapore does and what it means for
  the reader, never about parties or blame.
- H7. The kopitiam test. Read it aloud to a friend. If they would say "wait,
  really?", keep it. If they would shrug, rewrite.

## Rule 0 -- choose the fight before you phrase it

Phrasing cannot rescue a question nobody is arguing about.
- Start from the argument or the belief, not the data.
- Heavyweight rivals: if one side has no fans, there is no fight.
- Stakes the reader feels, not mechanisms.
- Push each side to its extreme so the reader can run the thought experiment.
A "fight" can also be the reader against a belief they hold (Jacob, 29 Sep:
friends think the Singapore dollar rises because the economy is strong).

Before writing any question, answer in one line each:
1. What do people in Singapore already believe or argue about this? (If nothing, stop.)
2. Who are the sides, or what is the belief and its rival explanation?
3. What does the whole country, or the ordinary reader, stand to gain or lose?
4. What is the extreme version of each side, and the everyday analogy for it?

## Rules for the sealed question (wording)

1. Two named rivals, or a claim to test.
2. A yardstick (in THESIS and the verdict; in the title only in the reader's words).
3. Contestable: a reasonable person could argue either answer.
4. Words the reader already owns in anything public. Precision goes in the lede.
5. Short: question under about 15 words; headline 60 characters max.
6. Exam forms for the sealed question: "Discuss whether", "To what extent",
   "Is X really...?", "Did X actually...?", "X or Y: which...?"
7. Question, then answer: the open page asks; the answer article's headline gives
   the finding, never a topic label.
8. Compliance wins: never what the reader should do; past tense for findings;
   only claims the data supports; thought experiments labelled.
9. Test before shipping: the kopitiam test (H7) for the title; "would it survive
   as a JC essay question?" for the sealed question.
